# A Pinch of Salt analysis

A Pinch of Salt — Robert Graves (1916)

This analysis of Robert Graves’s “A Pinch of Salt” explains its meaning, themes, bird imagery, rhyme, and advice about capturing poetic inspiration.

## The poem

```
When a dream is born in you
With a sudden clamorous pain,
When you know the dream is true
And lovely, with no flaw nor stain,
O then, be careful, or with sudden clutch
You'll hurt the delicate thing you prize so much.

Dreams are like a bird that mocks,
Flirting the feathers of his tail.
When you seize at the salt-box
Over the hedge you'll see him sail.
Old birds are neither caught with salt nor chaff:
They watch you from the apple bough and laugh.

Poet, never chase the dream.
Laugh yourself and turn away.
Mask your hunger, let it seem
Small matter if he come or stay;
But when he nestles in your hand at last,
Close up your fingers tight and hold him fast.
```

Text from [A Pinch of Salt](https://www.poetry.com/poem-analysis/31093/a-pinch-of-salt). Public domain in the US (Written around 1916 and published in Graves's Fairies and Fusiliers in 1918, this poem is in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The poem addresses a poet whose new dream arrives with painful intensity and apparent perfection. Graves compares that dream to a wary bird that flees when someone tries to catch it with a salt-box.

Its final stanza turns the comparison into advice: do not chase inspiration or show how badly you want it. Let the dream approach on its own, then hold it firmly when it finally settles in your hand.

## Meaning and interpretation

“A Pinch of Salt” treats poetic inspiration as both fragile and independent. The dream is real and beautiful, but an immediate clutch can damage the very thing the poet wants to preserve.

The poem’s apparent contradiction—feign indifference, then “hold him fast”—describes disciplined timing rather than passivity. Creative control comes from resisting panic first and acting decisively only when the idea is ready to be shaped.

## Themes

### Inspiration and creativity

The dream represents a poem or artistic idea that arrives suddenly, bringing both beauty and pain. Graves presents creation as something that cannot be summoned safely by force.

### Patience and restraint

The speaker’s commands to “never chase” and “turn away” make restraint the central practical lesson. The poet must conceal hunger long enough for the dream to come within reach.

### Desire and control

The poem does not reject possession; its final instruction is to close the fingers tightly. It distinguishes premature grasping, which hurts the dream, from deliberate control at the right moment.

## Form and meter

The poem consists of three six-line stanzas. Poetry.com identifies the rhyme pattern as ABABCC, DEDEFF, GHGHII, so each stanza moves from alternating rhymes into a closing couplet.

The lines are predominantly iambic tetrameter, though Graves varies the natural stresses in longer or more conversational phrases such as “O then, be careful” and “Close up your fingers tight.” The generally steady beat gives the advice a fable-like clarity.

The short stanzas and repeated couplet endings make the poem feel proverbial. The closing couplet of each stanza gathers the preceding image into a warning or instruction.

## Literary devices

### Extended metaphor

> Dreams are like a bird that mocks

The dream becomes a bird that can fly away, perch beyond reach, and eventually nestle in the poet’s hand. The sustained comparison makes inspiration seem alive rather than mechanical.

### Personification

> They watch you from the apple bough and laugh.

The old birds are given judgment and amusement. Their laughter turns failed capture into a lesson in humility: the dream understands the pursuer better than the pursuer understands the dream.

### Imperative voice

> Poet, never chase the dream.

Direct commands shift the poem from description to counsel. The address to “Poet” makes the lyric sound like a compact rule for artistic practice.

### Paradox

> Laugh yourself and turn away.

The speaker recommends pretending not to care about the dream even though the poem clearly values it. This tension captures the discipline of hiding urgency so desire does not become destructive.

## Historical context

Graves wrote the poem during the period identified in the catalog as around 1916; it appeared in Fairies and Fusiliers, published in 1918. Its public-domain status in the United States permits the full text here.

Patrick J. M. Quinn’s 1988 University of Warwick dissertation places “A Pinch of Salt” among the poems in Fairies and Fusiliers that were not created directly from Graves’s war experiences. That context helps explain why the poem turns from battlefield reality toward the problem of poetic escape and invention.

The poem’s salt-box and bird-catching image gives an old-fashioned rural fable shape to a modern artistic problem: how to receive an idea without either losing it or crushing it through anxious control.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of “A Pinch of Salt”?

The poem advises poets not to force inspiration. A dream or idea may be damaged by anxious pursuit, but it should be held firmly once it arrives naturally and is ready to shape.

### What does the bird symbolize in the poem?

The bird symbolizes a dream, poem, or creative insight that is beautiful but difficult to capture. Its flight represents the way inspiration escapes when the poet tries to seize it too soon.

### What are the main themes of “A Pinch of Salt”?

The main themes are artistic inspiration, patience, restraint, desire, and the difference between premature grasping and deliberate creative control.

### What is the form and meter of “A Pinch of Salt”?

It has three six-line stanzas with an ABABCC, DEDEFF, GHGHII rhyme pattern. The poem is predominantly written in iambic tetrameter, with natural variations in stress.
