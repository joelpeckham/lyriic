# A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning analysis

A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning — John Donne (1633)

This A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning analysis explains how John Donne turns physical separation into an argument for spiritual love, using gold, spheres, and a compass.

## The poem

```
As virtuous men pass mildly away,
And whisper to their souls to go,
Whilst some of their sad friends do say
The breath goes now, and some say, No:

So let us melt, and make no noise,
No tear-floods, nor sigh-tempests move;
'Twere profanation of our joys
To tell the laity our love.

Moving of th' earth brings harms and fears,
Men reckon what it did, and meant;
But trepidation of the spheres,
Though greater far, is innocent.

Dull sublunary lovers' love
(Whose soul is sense) cannot admit
Absence, because it doth remove
Those things which elemented it.

But we by a love so much refined,
That our selves know not what it is,
Inter-assured of the mind,
Care less, eyes, lips, and hands to miss.

Our two souls therefore, which are one,
Though I must go, endure not yet
A breach, but an expansion,
Like gold to airy thinness beat.

If they be two, they are two so
As stiff twin compasses are two;
Thy soul, the fixed foot, makes no show
To move, but doth, if the other do.

And though it in the center sit,
Yet when the other far doth roam,
It leans and hearkens after it,
And grows erect, as that comes home.

Such wilt thou be to me, who must,
Like th' other foot, obliquely run;
Thy firmness makes my circle just,
And makes me end where I begun.
```

Text from [A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning — full poem](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44131/a-valediction-forbidding-mourning). Public domain in the US (First published posthumously in 1633 in Songs and Sonnets, well before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The speaker asks his beloved to part quietly, comparing their calm farewell to the gentle death of virtuous men. He contrasts their refined love with ordinary love that depends on physical presence.

The poem then develops two images for enduring connection: souls expand like beaten gold, and the lovers resemble the joined legs of a compass. Distance becomes a widening circle that still returns to its starting point.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem argues that separation does not break a mature love grounded in mind and soul. The speaker does not deny physical absence; instead, he redefines it as an expansion that tests and reveals the relationship's strength.

The compass conceit makes fidelity geometric. The beloved's firmness lets the traveling speaker move in an apparently indirect arc while remaining oriented toward home, so the final circle promises both constancy and return.

## Themes

### Spiritual and physical love

The speaker distinguishes 'dull sublunary' love, which depends on the senses, from a rarer bond that remains secure when eyes, lips, and hands are apart. The distinction elevates mental and spiritual intimacy without pretending that the bodies are not separated.

### Distance and connection

Earthquakes, celestial spheres, beaten gold, and the compass all give physical distance a scale. The poem insists that separation can enlarge a bond rather than create a breach.

### Constancy and return

The fixed foot of the compass appears still but responds to the moving foot. Its firmness makes the circle 'just' and lets the speaker end where he began, turning travel into a figure for faithful return.

## Form and meter

The poem has nine quatrains, or 36 lines total. Each stanza uses alternating rhyme, producing the pattern ABAB CDCD EFEF and so on through the poem.

Its lines are chiefly iambic tetrameter, with four main beats, though Donne varies stress and phrasing for argument and speech. The regular quatrains contain a tightly controlled, conversational progression.

The poem's form supports its reasoning: each stanza advances a comparison or distinction, and the final two stanzas turn the compass image into a promise of stability and return.

## Literary devices

### Metaphysical conceit

> As stiff twin compasses are two;

The lovers' souls are compared to the legs of a drafting compass. The surprising mechanical image makes emotional fidelity visible as coordinated movement around a shared center.

### Simile

> Like gold to airy thinness beat.

Beaten gold becomes a model for love that stretches across distance without being divided. Its value also suggests that the relationship is precious, while the thinness keeps expansion from sounding effortless.

### Antithesis

> Moving of th' earth brings harms and fears, / But trepidation of the spheres

The poem contrasts noisy, visible earthly disturbance with the greater but 'innocent' motion of the heavens. The opposition prepares the distinction between ordinary and spiritually refined love.

### Personification

> It leans and hearkens after it

The fixed compass foot is given the actions of a waiting lover. Its apparent stillness becomes active attention, showing that constancy is responsive rather than passive.

## Historical context

The poem was probably written around 1611 or 1612 for Donne's wife, Anne More, before his journey to Continental Europe. The Poetry Foundation's text places it in Donne's posthumous poetic legacy, while Jack Lynch identifies its first publication as 1633.

It first appeared in the 1633 collection Songs and Sonnets. The title names a valediction, or farewell, but immediately forbids the public mourning that a conventional farewell might invite.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning?

The speaker says that a spiritually and mentally grounded love can survive physical separation. The farewell should be quiet because grief does not measure the strength of their bond.

### What does the compass conceit mean?

The beloved is the fixed foot and the speaker is the moving foot. Her firmness keeps his journey oriented and allows him to make a complete circle back home.

### What are the main themes of the poem?

Its main themes are spiritual love, distance, constancy, and return. Donne contrasts these with a more ordinary love dependent on physical sensation.

### What form and meter does the poem use?

It consists of nine quatrains with alternating rhyme and is chiefly written in iambic tetrameter, although Donne varies the rhythm to preserve a natural argumentative voice.
