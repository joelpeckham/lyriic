# A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning analysis

A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning — John Donne (1633)

Donne turns physical separation into an argument for spiritual love through gold, spheres, and a compass.

Full text from [A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44131/a-valediction-forbidding-mourning). Public domain in the US (First published posthumously in 1633; public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The speaker asks his beloved to part quietly, contrasting their refined love with ordinary love that depends on physical presence. Two images then explain endurance: souls expand like beaten gold and resemble compass legs.

```
So let us melt, and make no noise,
No tear-floods, nor sigh-tempests move;
```

Distance becomes an expansion that still returns home.[2]

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem argues that mature love survives separation because it is grounded in mind and soul rather than only the senses.[2]

```
Our two souls therefore, which are one,
Though I must go, endure not yet
A breach, but an expansion,
```

The compass makes fidelity geometric: the beloved’s firmness gives the traveling speaker direction.

## Themes

### Spiritual love

A refined bond remains secure when eyes, lips, and hands are apart.

### Distance and connection

```
Like gold to airy thinness beat.
```

Separation stretches the relationship without breaking it.

### Constancy and return

```
Thy firmness makes my circle just,
And makes me end where I begun.
```

The fixed foot’s steadiness makes return possible.

## Form and meter

Nine quatrains use alternating rhyme. The lines are chiefly iambic tetrameter, with variations that preserve an argumentative speaking voice.[3]

Each stanza advances a comparison, culminating in the compass conceit across the final three stanzas.

## Literary devices

### Metaphysical conceit

```
As stiff twin compasses are two;
Thy soul, the fixed foot, makes no show
```

The surprising mechanical comparison makes emotional fidelity visible as coordinated motion.

### Simile

Beaten gold and the compass offer physical models for a bond that expands but does not break.

### Antithesis

```
Moving of th' earth brings harms and fears,
But trepidation of the spheres,
```

Earthly disturbance and celestial movement prepare the contrast between ordinary and refined love.

## Historical context

The poem was probably written around 1611–12 for Donne’s wife before a journey and appeared in Songs and Sonnets in 1633. Its title frames a farewell while forbidding conventional public grief.[4]

```
Dull sublunary lovers' love
(Whose soul is sense) cannot admit
```

The vocabulary reflects Renaissance distinctions between earthly sensation and spiritual experience.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning?

A spiritually grounded love can survive physical separation without dramatic mourning.

### What does the compass conceit mean?

The beloved is the fixed foot; the traveling speaker remains connected and returns home.

### What form and meter does it use?

Nine quatrains with alternating rhyme and chiefly iambic tetrameter.
