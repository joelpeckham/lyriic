# Bless God, he went as soldiers analysis

Bless God, he went as soldiers — Emily Dickinson (1890)

This analysis follows the poem’s blend of military imagery, prayer, and conditional courage.

Full text from [Bless God, he went as soldiers](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Poems:_Third_Series/Bless_God,_he_went_as_soldiers). Public domain in the US (Written mid-nineteenth century and published posthumously in Poems, Third Series (1896), so the poem is public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The speaker imagines a beloved man entering battle with a musket and asks God to place him among the bravest blessed.

```
BLESS God, he went as soldiers,
His musket on his breast;
Grant, God, he charge the bravest
```

Seeing him in heavenly white would let the speaker face an unnamed foe without fear.[4]

## Meaning and interpretation

Faith is desired but not fully possessed. The speaker’s courage depends on seeing visible proof that the beloved has reached a safe, honored afterlife.[2]

```
Please God, might I behold him
In epauletted white,
I should not fear the foe then
```

The subjunctive “might” keeps the requested vision unresolved, so the poem ends before certainty arrives.[2]

## Themes

### Faith and uncertainty

Bless, Grant, and Please make belief sound like a request awaiting an answer rather than a settled creed.[4]

### Courage through example

The beloved’s imagined bravery becomes the speaker’s possible source of courage.

### War and spiritual language

Musket, charge, foe, and fight overlap with God, blest, and white, so secular battle and spiritual trial share one vocabulary.[2]

## Form and meter

Two quatrains broadly resemble common meter, with flexible stresses and hymn-like short lines.[1]

Slant rhyme links “breast”/“blest,” while “white”/“fight” gives the second stanza a restrained closure.

## Literary devices

### Martial metaphor

```
His musket on his breast;
```

Spiritual status is described through enlistment, rank, and battle.

### Color imagery

```
In epauletted white
```

Military insignia and heavenly whiteness combine in one desired vision.

### Anaphora

```
BLESS God, he went as soldiers,
Grant, God, he charge the bravest
Please God, might I behold him
```

Repeated divine address creates a prayer-like rhythm.

## Historical context

Dickinson preserved the poem in an early fascicle around 1859; it appeared posthumously in Poems, Third Series in 1896.[3]

Scholar Shira Wolosky notes that Dickinson used martial vocabulary before the Civil War; this poem leaves its foe and fight deliberately unnamed.[2]

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of “Bless God, he went as soldiers”?

The speaker wants a vision of a beloved soldier’s heavenly honor so she can face her own unnamed fight.

### What does “epauletted white” mean?

It joins decorated military clothing with heavenly white, imagining the beloved as both officer and blessed spirit.

### What is the poem’s form?

Two flexible, hymn-like quatrains that broadly resemble common meter, with slant rhyme.
