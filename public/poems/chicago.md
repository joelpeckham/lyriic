# Chicago analysis

Chicago — Carl Sandburg (1914)

Sandburg admits Chicago’s violence and hunger before celebrating its labor, energy, and civic pride.

Full text from [Chicago](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poetrymagazine/poems/12840/chicago). Public domain in the US (First published in Poetry in 1914, before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The speaker names Chicago through industrial occupations, concedes its vice and brutality, then answers its critics with an image of a laughing worker.

```
Hog Butcher for the World,
Tool Maker, Stacker of Wheat,
Player with Railroads and the Nation's Freight Handler;
```

The city’s greatness is grounded in work rather than polished respectability.

## Meaning and interpretation

The defense persuades because it does not pretend Chicago is innocent. Pride and social damage coexist in the same urban body.

```
Come and show me another city with lifted head singing so proud to be alive and coarse and strong and cunning.
```

The final laughter makes resilience a public, physical act.[2]

## Themes

### Industrial labor

The opening catalogue identifies Chicago through slaughtering, tools, grain, and freight.

### Clear-eyed civic pride

Crime and hunger are admitted before the speaker celebrates the city.

### Youth and resilience

```
Building, breaking, rebuilding,
Under the smoke, dust all over his mouth, laughing with white teeth,
```

Rebuilding turns hardship into continued motion.

## Form and meter

This is free verse with no fixed stanza, rhyme scheme, or regular meter. Its form comes from catalogues, punctuation, and changing line length.

Long sentences are interrupted by one-word action lines such as “Shoveling” and “Laughing!”, creating a physical rhythm.

## Literary devices

### Personification

```
here is a tall bold slugger set vivid against the little soft cities;
```

Chicago becomes a working fighter with sweat, teeth, ribs, and laughter.

### Catalogue

```
Bareheaded,
Shoveling,
Wrecking,
```

Accumulated occupations make civic identity collective.

### Repetition

```
Laughing!
Laughing the stormy, husky, brawling laughter of Youth,
```

Repeated laughter gathers force at the close.

## Historical context

Sandburg moved to Chicago in 1912 and worked as a reporter. The National Park Service connects his early writing with labor rights and unsafe working conditions.[2]

Harriet Monroe published the poem in Poetry in March 1914; it later appeared in Chicago Poems (1916).[3]

## Common questions

### What is the meaning?

Chicago celebrates industrial strength and working people without denying crime, poverty, or corruption.

### Why Big Shoulders?

The phrase personifies the city as a strong laborer carrying collective work and burden.

### What form is used?

Free verse shaped by repetition, catalogues, and long and short lines.
