# Each and All analysis

Each and All — Ralph Waldo Emerson (1839)

This Each and All analysis explains how Ralph Waldo Emerson turns a bird, seashells, and a lover’s bride into a meditation on beauty, perception, and the perfect whole.

## The poem

```
Little thinks, in the field, yon red-cloaked clown,
Of thee from the hill-top looking down;
The heifer that lows in the upland farm,
Far-heard, lows not thine ear to charm;
The sexton, tolling his bell at noon,
Deems not that great Napoleon
Stops his horse, and lists with delight,
Whilst his files sweep round yon Alpine height;
Nor knowest thou what argument
Thy life to thy neighbor’s creed has lent.
All are needed by each one;
Nothing is fair or good alone.
I thought the sparrow’s note from heaven,
Singing at dawn on the alder bough;
I brought him home, in his nest, at even;
He sings the song, but it pleases not now,
For I did not bring home the river and sky;
He sang to my ear,—they sang to my eye.
The delicate shells lay on the shore;
The bubbles of the latest wave
Fresh pearls to their enamel gave;
And the bellowing of the savage sea
Greeted their safe escape to me.
I wiped away the weeds and foam,
I fetched my sea-born treasures home;
But the poor, unsightly, noisome things
Had left their beauty on the shore,
With the sun, and the sand, and the wild uproar.
The lover watched his graceful maid,
As ’mid the virgin train she stayed,
Nor knew her beauty’s best attire
Was woven still by the snow-white choir.
At last she came to his hermitage,
Like the bird from the woodlands to the cage;—
The gay enchantment was undone,
A gentle wife, but fairy none.
Then I said, ‘I covet truth;
Beauty is unripe childhood’s cheat;
I leave it behind with the games of youth:’—
As I spoke, beneath my feet
The ground-pine curled its pretty wreath,
Running over the club-moss burrs;
I inhaled the violet’s breath;
Around me stood the oaks and firs;
Pine-cones and acorns lay on the ground;
Over me soared the eternal sky,
Full of light and of deity;
Again I saw, again I heard,
The rolling river, the morning bird;—
Beauty through my senses stole;
I yielded myself to the perfect whole.
```

Text from [Each and All by Ralph Waldo Emerson](https://poets.org/poem/each-and-all). Public domain in the US (First published in 1839, Each and All is in the public domain in the United States; the Academy of American Poets marks the poem as public domain.).

## Summary

The speaker begins with a series of scenes in which people, animals, and events affect one another without fully knowing it. The conclusion of that opening claim is that nothing is fair or good alone.

Three examples then test the idea: a sparrow loses its meaning indoors, shells lose their beauty away from the shore, and a woman’s enchantment disappears when the lover brings her into his hermitage. When the speaker finally returns to the whole natural scene, beauty comes back through all the senses.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem’s central meaning is that beauty is relational rather than portable. The sparrow still sings and the shells remain physically present, but the river, sky, sun, sand, and sea that made them beautiful cannot be carried home with them.

The final movement complicates the speaker’s attempt to reject beauty in favor of truth. As PoetryVerse observes, the ending is not simply a choice of “beauty” over “truth”; it presents truth as inseparable from the whole sensory world. Yielding, rather than possessing, becomes the speaker’s way of understanding.

## Themes

### Organic unity

The refrain-like claim “All are needed by each one” makes each image part of a larger system. A person’s life, a bird’s song, and a landscape acquire significance through relationships that exceed the individual object.

### Beauty and context

The poem repeatedly removes something admired from its setting and records the loss. Beauty is shown as an event involving sound, light, place, motion, and memory, not as a permanent possession stored inside a thing.

### Possession versus participation

The speaker’s instinct is to bring the bird and shells home and to draw the beloved into a private hermitage. Each act of possession isolates what was admired. The ending reverses that movement when the speaker yields to the surrounding world.

### Limits of individual perspective

The opening images show that people are often unaware of the meanings their actions have for others. Emerson’s wider point is social as well as natural: each life contributes to a pattern that no single observer can completely see.

## Form and meter

Each and All is a 51-line poem presented as one continuous block rather than as separately titled stanzas. The poem’s argument unfolds through linked examples, so the uninterrupted shape reinforces its movement from parts toward a whole.

The dominant rhyme pattern is paired end rhyme, beginning with “clown”/“down” and “farm”/“charm.” Emerson varies the pattern with occasional half-rhyme, including “one”/“alone,” while internal pauses keep the long sentences conversational.

The lines use a flexible, songlike accentual rhythm rather than a single meter maintained without variation. Because the poem shifts between short statements, narrative lines, and descriptive lists, the open editor settings are more useful than forcing it into a fixed meter.

## Literary devices

### Refrain-like aphorism

> All are needed by each one; / Nothing is fair or good alone.

These compact lines state the poem’s governing principle after the opening examples. Their balanced syntax makes a philosophical claim sound memorable and almost proverbial.

### Sensory imagery

> The bubbles of the latest wave / Fresh pearls to their enamel gave;

Visual, auditory, and tactile details make the shore an active environment. When the shells are removed, the poem shows that their beauty included the sea’s motion and sound, not only their surfaces.

### Simile

> Like the bird from the woodlands to the cage;—

The comparison links the lover’s domestic ideal to the earlier act of caging the sparrow. It gives the poem’s romantic example an ethical tension: intimacy can become another form of removal.

### Anaphora and accumulation

> Again I saw, again I heard, / The rolling river, the morning bird;—

Repeated openings such as “The,” “I,” and “Again” gather separate details into a dense final perception. The accumulating list enacts the speaker’s renewed awareness of interdependence.

## Historical context

Each and All was first published in the Western Messenger on February 6, 1839, after being composed in the 1830s. Its public-domain status is confirmed by the Academy of American Poets.

Emerson was an American essayist, philosopher, and poet associated with Transcendentalism. The poem’s emphasis on nature, intuition, and the relation between individual parts and a larger unity aligns with concerns that also appear in his prose.

The poem’s language combines moral reflection with ordinary New England scenes and a striking reference to Napoleon in the Alps. That scale shift—from a field and farm to European history—helps establish the idea that local actions can participate in meanings their actors never intend.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of “Each and All”?

The poem argues that beauty and meaning depend on relationships. A bird, shell, or person loses something when separated from the landscape, community, and sensory conditions that give it significance.

### What are the main themes in “Each and All”?

Its main themes are organic unity, beauty in context, the limits of individual perception, and the danger of treating nature or other people as possessions.

### What is the form and meter of “Each and All”?

It is a 51-line block-form poem with paired end rhymes and a flexible, songlike rhythm. Its varied line lengths and changing syntax make it better understood as irregular accentual verse than as one fixed meter.

### Why does the speaker bring the sparrow and shells home?

He tries to possess and preserve things whose beauty impressed him in nature. Once removed from the river, sky, shore, sun, sand, and sea, they remain materially present but no longer produce the same experience.
