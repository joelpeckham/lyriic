# Each and All analysis

Each and All — Ralph Waldo Emerson (1839)

Emerson shows that beauty and meaning depend on the living whole surrounding each object.

Full text from [Each and All](https://poets.org/poem/each-and-all). Public domain in the US (First published in 1839, before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

A sparrow, shells, and a lover’s bride lose their beauty when removed from the contexts that made them meaningful.[1]

```
All are needed by each one;
Nothing is fair or good alone.
```

The speaker finally returns to the whole natural scene and yields to it.

## Meaning and interpretation

Beauty is relational rather than portable: the river, sky, sun, sand, and sea cannot be carried home with their objects.

```
For I did not bring home the river and sky;
He sang to my ear,—they sang to my eye.
```

Possession gives way to participation.[2]

## Themes

### Organic unity

Each object participates in a larger system of natural and social meanings.

### Beauty and context

```
Had left their beauty on the shore,
With the sun, and the sand, and the wild uproar.
```

Beauty includes place, sound, light, and motion.

### Possession versus participation

The speaker’s attempts to bring things home isolate what he admired; the ending reverses that movement.

## Form and meter

The 51-line poem unfolds as a continuous argument through linked examples, using flexible songlike rhythm.

Paired end rhymes and conversational pauses give the long narrative a measured movement.

## Literary devices

### Aphorism

```
All are needed by each one;
Nothing is fair or good alone.
```

Balanced syntax turns the governing idea into a memorable principle.

### Sensory imagery

```
The bubbles of the latest wave
Fresh pearls to their enamel gave;
```

The shore’s beauty is environmental, not merely visual.

### Simile

```
Like the bird from the woodlands to the cage;—
```

The romantic example echoes the earlier caged sparrow.

## Historical context

The poem was published in the Western Messenger in 1839 and reflects Emerson’s Transcendentalist interest in nature and unity.[2]

Its local field, shore, and woodland scenes expand into a philosophy of interdependence.
