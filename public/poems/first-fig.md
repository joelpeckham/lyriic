# First Fig analysis

First Fig — Edna St. Vincent Millay (1920)

This First Fig analysis explains how Edna St. Vincent Millay uses a double-burning candle, common meter, and defiant address to frame an intense life as brief but beautiful.

## The poem

```
My candle burns at both ends;
It will not last the night;
But ah, my foes, and oh, my friends—
It gives a lovely light!
```

Text from [First Fig by Edna St. Vincent Millay](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poetrymagazine/poems/14095/first-fig). Public domain in the US (The poem first appeared in Poetry in 1918 and was published in A Few Figs from Thistles in 1920; the Academy of American Poets identifies it as public domain.).

## Summary

The speaker compares their life to a candle burning at both ends. That choice promises a shorter duration, but it produces an especially vivid light.

In four compact lines, the speaker accepts both admiration and opposition. Friends and foes are invited to watch the flame, while the speaker refuses to apologize for its intensity.

## Meaning and interpretation

The candle is an extended metaphor for a life spent with unusual energy, appetite, or creative and erotic freedom. The poem does not specify one behavior, so the image remains open to several forms of wholehearted living.

The final exclamation changes the apparent warning about exhaustion into a celebration. Millay’s speaker values the quality and brightness of experience even while acknowledging that it cannot last.

## Themes

### Intensity and transience

A candle burning at both ends consumes itself quickly, making brilliance inseparable from limitation. The poem treats a short, vivid life as a meaningful alternative to a longer but more restrained one.

### Independence and defiance

By addressing “my foes” and “my friends” together, the speaker places public judgment beneath personal choice. Approval and disapproval matter less than the speaker’s decision to live fully.

### Beauty in the present

The last line concentrates attention on what the flame gives now: “a lovely light.” The poem’s perspective favors immediate experience over promises about the future.

## Form and meter

“First Fig” is a single quatrain with the compact, alternating movement of common meter: long and short lines built from iambic tetrameter and trimeter. The poem is also a ballad-stanza variation rather than a perfectly regular exercise.

The end rhymes form an ABAB pattern: “ends”/“friends” and “night”/“light.” The neat rhyme and quick rhythm make the speaker’s declaration memorable and almost songlike.

The opening line bends the expected rhythm with seven syllables and the forceful stresses of “both ends.” That compression makes the line sound abruptly cut short, echoing the life the speaker says will not last the night.

## Literary devices

### Extended metaphor

> My candle burns at both ends;

The candle stands for the speaker’s life or way of living. Burning it at both ends joins brilliance to self-consumption, so the image carries both pleasure and risk.

### Apostrophe

> But ah, my foes, and oh, my friends—

The speaker turns outward to address absent or generalized listeners. Naming enemies and friends together makes the declaration public and emphasizes that neither group controls the speaker’s choices.

### Alliteration and sound pattern

> My candle burns at both ends

The repeated b sound gives the opening force and momentum. In the last line, the softer pairing of “lovely light” makes the flame sound attractive as well as dangerous.

### Caesura and punctuation

> But ah, my foes, and oh, my friends—

The semicolon, comma, and dash create pauses that shape the speaker’s performance. The pauses slow the address just before the emphatic, exclamatory conclusion.

## Historical context

“First Fig” appeared in Poetry in 1918 and became the opening poem of A Few Figs from Thistles, published in book form in 1920. The poem therefore belongs to Millay’s early public career and to the cultural changes associated with the start of the 1920s.

The collection’s frank treatment of unconventional women’s lives and sexuality helped make Millay a prominent Greenwich Village literary figure. The poem’s speaker can be read in that context, but the text itself leaves the candle’s exact meaning deliberately unspecified.

The poem’s familiar candle image also gives its modern subject a traditional lyric frame. Its regular rhyme and meter make the speaker’s rebellious claim sound polished, witty, and easy to remember.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of “First Fig”?

The poem celebrates living intensely and independently even when that life may be brief or exhausting. Its speaker values a bright present over a safer, more restrained existence.

### What does the candle symbolize in “First Fig”?

The candle symbolizes the speaker’s life and possibly creative, social, or erotic energy. Burning at both ends suggests using that energy quickly and fully.

### What is the form and meter of “First Fig”?

It is a four-line quatrain using a common-meter or ballad-stanza pattern, with alternating iambic tetrameter and trimeter and an ABAB rhyme scheme. The first line includes a deliberate rhythmic compression.

### Why does the speaker mention both foes and friends?

The paired address shows that the speaker expects mixed reactions but refuses to let either praise or criticism determine how they live. Both groups are asked to witness the same bright, temporary flame.
