# Go and Catch a Falling Star analysis

Song: Go and catch a falling star — John Donne (1633)

This analysis explains how Donne turns supernatural commands and a brisk song form into a bitter argument about fidelity.

Full text from [Song — full poem](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Song:_Go_and_catch_a_falling_star). Public domain in the US (First published in Donne's collected poems in 1633, well before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The speaker orders a listener to perform impossible tasks, then asks him to search for a woman who is both true and fair.[2]

```
GO and catch a falling star,
Get with child a mandrake root,
Tell me where all past years are,
```

The final stanza withdraws the apparent invitation: even if such a woman were found, she would be false before the speaker arrived.[3]

## Meaning and interpretation

Donne makes fidelity seem less attainable than catching a star or hearing mermaids. The comic quest turns a conventional complaint about infidelity into a memorable paradox.[2]

```
And swear
No where
Lives a woman true and fair.
```

The speaker’s misogynistic generalization is a historical literary posture, not a neutral account of relationships; the song’s musical confidence makes it sound persuasive.[2]

## Themes

### Fidelity and skepticism

The search for a faithful woman becomes an impossible quest, and the ending turns skepticism into a universal judgment.[2]

### Gender and power

The poem treats women’s supposed inconstancy as a conventionally gendered claim that modern readers can examine critically.[2]

### Wonder and disillusionment

Stars, mandrakes, mermaids, and a ten-thousand-day journey create marvels against which human constancy is judged.

## Form and meter

The poem has three nine-line stanzas. Each uses ABABCC followed by a rhyming triplet, giving the argument brisk song-like momentum.[3]

Its predominant rhythm is trochaic tetrameter; the short “And find / What wind” lines sharpen each stanza’s punchline.[3]

## Literary devices

### Hyperbole

```
Ride ten thousand days and nights
Till Age snow white hairs on thee;
```

The enormous journey makes fidelity sound beyond ordinary human effort.

### Imperative

```
GO and catch a falling star
```

The direct command gives the poem the energy of spoken banter.

### Folkloric allusion

```
Teach me to hear mermaids singing,
Or to keep off envy's stinging,
```

Supernatural references build a catalogue of strange possibilities.

### Irony

```
Such a pilgrimage were sweet.
Yet do not; I would not go,
```

The reversal exposes the gap between stated desire and settled disappointment.

## Historical context

The poem was first published posthumously in 1633 in the first collected edition of Donne’s poems. Its early-modern song form supports a witty complaint about inconstancy.[3]

The speaker’s claim belongs to a gendered literary convention and can be read both as clever rhetoric and as prejudice.[2]

## Common questions

### What is the poem’s main meaning?

The speaker claims that a beautiful, faithful woman is as impossible to find as a supernatural wonder, though the claim is deliberately witty and gendered.

### What are its main themes?

Fidelity, skepticism, wonder, gender, and disillusionment shape the poem’s argument.

### What form does it use?

It has three nine-line stanzas with ABABCC and a closing triplet, in a brisk, predominantly trochaic song rhythm.
