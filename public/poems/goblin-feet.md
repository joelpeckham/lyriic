# Goblin Feet analysis

Goblin Feet — J. R. R. Tolkien (1915)

Read J. R. R. Tolkien’s Goblin Feet as a spell-like journey into a small, vivid fairy world. This analysis explains the poem’s meaning, imagery, sound, form, and final turn from wonder to loss.

## The poem

```
I am off down the road
Where the fairy lanterns glowed
And the little pretty flitter-mice are flying:
A slender band of grey
It runs creepily away
And the hedges and the grasses are a-sighing.
The air is full of wings,
And of blundery beetle-things
That warn you with their whirring and their humming.
O! I hear the tiny horns
Of enchanted leprechauns
And the padded feet of many gnomes a-coming!

O! the lights: O! the gleams:
O! the little twinkly sounds:
O! the rustle of their noiseless little robes:
O! the echo of their feet—of their happy little feet:
O! the swinging lamps in the starlit globes.

I must follow in their train
Down the crooked fairy lane
Where the coney-rabbits long ago have gone.
And where silvery they sing
In a moving moonlit ring
All a twinkle with the jewels they have on.
They are fading round the turn
Where the glow worms palely burn
And the echo of their padding feet is dying!
O! it’s knocking at my heart—
Let me go! O! let me start!
For the little magic hours are all a-flying.

O! the warmth! O! the hum!
O! the colours in the dark!
O! the gauzy wings of golden honey-flies!
O! the music of their feet—of their dancing goblin feet!
O! the magic! O! the sorrow when it dies.
```

Text from [Goblin Feet — Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Goblin_Feet). Public domain in the US (First published in Oxford Poetry 1915, Goblin Feet is in the public domain in the United States because it was published before January 1, 1931.).

## Summary

The speaker follows a procession of tiny supernatural beings through a moonlit landscape. Fairy lanterns, flying creatures, glow-worms, jewels, and padded feet turn an ordinary road into a hidden world that seems to invite participation.

The poem’s delight is inseparable from its disappearance. The procession fades around a turn, and the speaker’s urgent wish to follow ends with the recognition that magic brings sorrow when it dies.

## Meaning and interpretation

Goblin Feet presents enchantment as an experience of sensory abundance: the speaker hears humming, horns, rustling robes, and rhythmic feet while seeing lamps, colours, jewels, and wings. The repeated exclamations make wonder feel immediate and almost breathless.

The poem also understands wonder as temporary. The speaker is drawn after the fairies, but the central movement is away from the speaker: their echo dies, the magic hours fly, and the final line names the grief produced by their passing. The meaning is therefore not simply that fairy life is beautiful; it is that beauty becomes poignant because it cannot be held.

## Themes

### Wonder and enchantment

The poem builds a complete fairy atmosphere from light, sound, movement, and diminutive beings. Its catalog of sensations makes the supernatural world feel close enough to enter.

### Transience and loss

The fairies fade, their footsteps become an echo, and the speaker’s excitement turns to sorrow. The poem treats enchantment as something whose departure is part of its emotional power.

### Desire to cross into another world

The repeated wish to follow, go, and start turns observation into pursuit. The speaker is not satisfied with seeing the fairy procession; they want to leave the ordinary road behind and join it.

## Form and meter

The poem has four stanzas, alternating a twelve-line descriptive stanza with a six-line exclamatory stanza. Its lines use a flexible accentual-syllabic rhythm rather than one fixed meter, so the poem feels closer to a song or incantation than to a strict metrical exercise.

End rhyme and internal sound patterning help hold the irregular lines together: “flying” answers “a-sighing,” while “humming” answers “a-coming.” The short exclamatory lines in the second and fourth stanzas create choruses, and the repeated “O!” gives the poem a breathless, performative pulse.

Because the rhythm changes with the speaker’s excitement, an open editor setting is more useful than imposing a single meter. Counts can still help readers notice how Tolkien varies line length for speed, emphasis, and musical effect.

## Literary devices

### Alliteration and onomatopoeia

> “their whirring and their humming”

The repeated consonants and sound-imitating words make the invisible fairy traffic audible. The poem asks readers to hear the world before they can fully see it.

### Sensory imagery

> “O! the colours in the dark!”

Visual, auditory, and tactile details accumulate across the poem. The fairies are defined less by explanation than by the sensations surrounding their movement.

### Repetition and refrain-like exclamation

> “O! the echo of their feet—of their happy little feet”

Repeated words such as “little,” “feet,” and “O!” establish a childlike, incantatory voice. The repetitions also make the final “sorrow” feel like a deliberate reversal of the earlier delight.

### Personification

> “the hedges and the grasses are a-sighing”

The landscape participates in the fairy world. Plants, insects, lamps, and echoes all seem animated, so the road becomes a responsive threshold rather than a passive setting.

## Historical context

Goblin Feet was written on April 27–28, 1915, and published in Oxford Poetry 1915 by B. H. Blackwell in December 1915. Tolkien Library identifies it as one of Tolkien’s early fairy poems and lists Tolkien among the volume’s contributors.

The Tolkien Library account says Tolkien wrote the poem to please Edith Bratt, who loved “spring and flowers and trees, and little elfin people.” It describes the poem as closely aligned with the Victorian depiction of fairies, which helps explain its small scale, bright enchantment, and emphasis on delicate movement.

The poem also records an early stage in Tolkien’s changing conception of fairies. Tolkien later disliked the work’s diminutive fairy tradition; a reported 1971 response called it “the unhappy little thing” he wished could be buried. That later reaction adds historical tension, but it does not erase the poem’s carefully sustained music and atmosphere.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of Goblin Feet?

The poem presents enchantment as beautiful but temporary. The speaker longs to follow a fairy procession, then feels sorrow when its music, warmth, and footsteps fade.

### What are the main themes in Goblin Feet?

The main themes are wonder, fairy enchantment, sensory imagination, the desire to enter another world, and the sadness created by the passing of beauty.

### What form and meter does Goblin Feet use?

It has four alternating twelve-line and six-line stanzas. Its rhythm is flexible and songlike rather than strictly regular, with rhyme, alliteration, repetition, and exclamations creating its incantatory movement.

### Why did Tolkien later dislike Goblin Feet?

Tolkien later rejected the poem’s small, Victorian-style fairy imagery, which differed from the more substantial and mythic conception of elves he developed afterward. His later dislike is part of the poem’s history, not a substitute for reading its language.
