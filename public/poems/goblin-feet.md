# Goblin Feet analysis

Goblin Feet — J. R. R. Tolkien (1915)

This analysis reads Tolkien’s poem as a spell-like journey from sensory enchantment to loss.

Full text from [Goblin Feet — full poem](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Goblin_Feet). Public domain in the US (First published in Oxford Poetry 1915, before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The speaker follows a procession of tiny supernatural beings through a moonlit landscape of lanterns, wings, jewels, and padded feet.[2]

```
I am off down the road
Where the fairy lanterns glowed
And the little pretty flitter-mice are flying:
```

The procession fades around a turn, so delight becomes sorrow when the magic disappears.[2]

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem makes enchantment sensory: the reader hears humming, horns, robes, and feet while seeing lamps, colours, jewels, and wings.[2]

```
They are fading round the turn
Where the glow worms palely burn
And the echo of their padding feet is dying!
```

Wonder is temporary. The speaker wants to follow into another world, but the final line admits that magic’s disappearance is part of its emotional power.[2]

## Themes

### Wonder

Repeated lights, wings, and diminutive creatures make the fairy world feel close enough to enter.

### Transience and loss

Fading figures and dying echoes turn enchantment into an experience of grief.

### Desire for another world

Commands such as “Let me go!” turn observation into pursuit and longing.

## Form and meter

The four stanzas alternate longer descriptive passages with short exclamatory choruses. Flexible accentual-syllabic rhythm makes the poem songlike rather than strictly metrical.

Rhyme, internal sound, repeated “O!”, and recurring words such as “little” and “feet” create an incantatory pulse.[2]

## Literary devices

### Onomatopoeia

```
That warn you with their whirring and their humming.
```

Sound-imitating words make the invisible fairy traffic audible.

### Sensory imagery

```
O! the warmth! O! the hum!
O! the colours in the dark!
```

Accumulated sensations define the fairies more vividly than explanation.

### Repetition

```
O! the echo of their feet—of their happy little feet:
```

Repetition establishes a childlike, spell-like voice.

## Historical context

Tolkien wrote the poem on April 27–28, 1915, to please Edith Bratt, and Oxford Poetry 1915 published it in December.[3]

The Tolkien Library describes its fairy world as close to the Victorian depiction of fairies; Tolkien later regretted the poem’s diminutive tradition.[2]

## Common questions

### What is Goblin Feet about?

It depicts a speaker following a fairy procession whose beauty and music fade into sorrow.

### What are its themes?

Wonder, sensory imagination, transience, loss, and the desire to cross into another world.

### What form does it use?

Its flexible, songlike rhythm alternates longer descriptions with exclamatory choruses.
