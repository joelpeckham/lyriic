# Goliath and David analysis

Goliath and David — Robert Graves (1916)

Graves turns the biblical victory into a grim First World War parable; this analysis follows its reversal and war imagery.

Full text from [The Muse in Arms — full poem](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Muse_in_Arms/In_Memoriam). Public domain in the US (First published in 1916, before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

Graves retells David and Goliath but announces that the familiar historian “Had not the heart to tell it right.”[2]

```
But the historian of that fight
Had not the heart to tell it right.
```

David’s sling and staff fail against shield and steel; Goliath survives and stands over the dead boy.[1]

## Meaning and interpretation

The reversal challenges the comforting underdog myth: courage cannot cancel superior material force in modern combat.[2]

```
(God's eyes are dim, His ears are shut.)
One cruel backhand sabre-cut—
```

The parenthesis makes the failure theological as well as military, while the dedication turns the rewritten legend into a memorial.[3]

## Themes

### Failure of heroic myth

The expected miracle is replaced by a blunt account of defeat.

### Unequal power

Pebble and wood are opposed to shield, armor, and steel.[2]

### Faith and divine silence

David trusts that God will save him, but the poem depicts no intervention.

### Grief and remembrance

The dedication identifies the poem with David Cuthbert Thomas, killed at Fricourt in March 1916.[3]

## Form and meter

The poem has a twelve-line opening stanza and a thirty-four-line battle stanza, mostly organized in rhyming couplets.[2]

The brisk couplets accommodate narrative action, while interruptions such as “Then ... but” fracture the traditional tale at the moment of reversal.

## Literary devices

### Biblical inversion

```
ONCE an earlier David took
Smooth pebbles from the brook:
```

The reversed title and altered outcome keep the biblical story present while denying its familiar moral.

### Onomatopoeia

```
Goliath's shield parries each cast.
Clang! clang! and clang! was David's last.
```

Metallic repetition makes the failed attack audible.

### Simile

```
The pebble, humming from the sling
Like a wild bee, flies a sure line
```

The lively simile emphasizes the pebble’s smallness against the giant’s equipment.

## Historical context

The poem is dedicated “For D. C. T., killed at Fricourt, March 1916”; the GMU notes identify David Cuthbert Thomas as Graves’s friend and fellow Royal Welch Fusilier.[3]

The spike-helmeted giant brings the biblical encounter into the First World War and its German military imagery.[1]

## Common questions

### What is the poem’s meaning?

It reverses the biblical victory to show how courage and faith can fail against overwhelming force.

### What are its themes?

Heroic myth, unequal power, divine silence, war, grief, and remembrance.

### What form does it use?

A two-stanza narrative built mainly from rhyming couplets, with varied meter and abrupt sound effects.
