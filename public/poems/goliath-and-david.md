# Goliath and David analysis

Goliath and David — Robert Graves (1916)

Robert Graves’s Goliath and David turns a familiar biblical victory into a grim First World War parable. This analysis explains the poem’s meaning, themes, form, and war imagery.

## The poem

```
ONCE an earlier David took
Smooth pebbles from the brook:
Out between the lines he went
To that one-sided tournament,
A shepherd boy who stood out fine
And young to fight a Philistine
Clad all in brazen mail. He swears
That he's killed lions, he's killed bears,
And those that scorn the God of Zion
Shall perish so like bear or lion.
But the historian of that fight
Had not the heart to tell it right.

Striding within javelin range
Goliath marvels at this strange
Goodly-faced boy so proud of strength.
David's clear eye measures the length;
With hand thrust back, he cramps one knee,
Poises a moment thoughtfully,
And hurls with a long vengeful swing.
The pebble, humming from the sling
Like a wild bee, flies a sure line
For the forehead of the Philistine,
Then ... but there comes a brazen clink,
And quicker than a man can think
Goliath's shield parries each cast.
Clang! clang! and clang! was David's last.
Scorn blazes in the Giant's eye,
Towering unhurt six cubits high.
Says foolish David, "Damn your shield,
And damn my sling, but I'll not yield."
He takes his staff of Mamre oak,
A knotted shepherd-staff that's broke
The skull of many a wolf and fox
Come filching lambs from Jesse's flocks.
Loud laughs Goliath, and that laugh
Can scatter chariots like blown chaff
To rout; but David, calm and brave,
Holds his ground, for God will save.
Steel crosses wood, a flash, and oh!
Shame for Beauty's overthrow!
(God's eyes are dim, His ears are shut.)
One cruel backhand sabre-cut—
"I'm hit, I'm killed," young David cries,
Throws blindly forward, chokes ... and dies.
And look, spike-helmeted, grey, grim,
Goliath straddles over him.
```

Text from [The Muse in Arms: “Goliath and David”](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Muse_in_Arms/In_Memoriam). Public domain in the US (First published in 1916, Goliath and David is in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The poem retells the biblical encounter between David and Goliath, but its “historian” admits that the familiar account was not told honestly. David enters the fight with a sling, a shepherd’s staff, courage, and confidence that God will save him.

The expected miracle never arrives. Goliath’s shield defeats the pebbles, his sabre defeats the staff, and the final image of the giant standing over David replaces heroic triumph with the blunt reality of wartime death.

## Meaning and interpretation

Graves uses the reversal to challenge the comforting logic of the underdog myth. David is brave and resourceful, but courage cannot overcome an overwhelming material advantage: armor, shield, and steel. The poem’s meaning is therefore less that bravery is worthless than that traditional stories can conceal the costs and randomness of real combat.

The parenthetical line “(God's eyes are dim, His ears are shut.)” makes the reversal theological as well as military. David trusts divine rescue, yet the battlefield offers no confirming miracle. The dedication to David Cuthbert Thomas, killed at Fricourt in March 1916, gives the rewritten myth the weight of a personal memorial.

## Themes

### The failure of heroic myth

The poem begins in the shape of the familiar Bible story, then exposes its omission: “the historian of that fight / Had not the heart to tell it right.” The altered ending insists that inherited narratives of victory may be unable to represent modern slaughter.

### War and unequal power

David’s pebble and wooden staff are set against Goliath’s shield and sabre. The contrast turns the legendary contest into a practical military imbalance in which individual courage cannot cancel superior force.

### Faith and divine silence

David “Holds his ground, for God will save,” but the parenthesis immediately undercuts that confidence. God’s dim eyes and shut ears suggest a world in which faith remains emotionally powerful while offering no protection from violence.

### Grief and remembrance

The dedication identifies the poem with David Cuthbert Thomas, a friend of Graves who was killed at Fricourt. The biblical name becomes a memorial frame: the poem remembers a young man by placing his death inside a story that readers expect to end differently.

## Form and meter

The poem has two stanzas: a twelve-line opening that establishes the revised legend, followed by a thirty-four-line battle narrative. Its lengthened second stanza makes the failed attack and its consequences unfold in close detail.

The lines are organized mainly as rhyming couplets, with frequent masculine end rhymes and a brisk, song-like forward motion. The meter is not mechanically regular; Graves varies line length and stress to accommodate action, speech, and sudden violence. Poem Analysis identifies the dominant pattern as aabbccdd rhyme within the poem’s structured couplets.

The opening phrase “ONCE an earlier David” sounds like a traditional tale, while the interruption “Then ... but” and the final clipped cries fracture that storytelling rhythm. The poem’s formal control makes the brutal reversal feel deliberate rather than accidental.

## Literary devices

### Biblical allusion and inversion

> “Goliath and David”

The title reverses the conventional order “David and Goliath,” signaling that the giant, not the shepherd, will dominate. Allusions to Zion, Jesse, Mamre, and the earlier fight keep the biblical story present while Graves changes its moral outcome.

### Sound imagery and onomatopoeia

> “Clang! clang! and clang! was David's last.”

The repeated metallic word makes the shield’s defense audible and mechanical. It replaces the single miraculous impact of the traditional story with a sequence of failed blows, then turns sound into a marker of David’s death.

### Simile and visual contrast

> “Like a wild bee”

The pebble’s humming flight sounds quick and alive, but the comparison also makes it small. That fragile image is set against the brazen shield, steel, and the giant’s towering body.

### Irony

> “Holds his ground, for God will save.”

The confident clause is immediately contradicted by David’s death. The irony exposes the gap between the promise expected from the old legend and the silence Graves associates with the modern battlefield.

## Historical context

The poem is dedicated “For D. C. T., killed at Fricourt, March 1916.” Robert Graves’s friend David Cuthbert Thomas served with him and Siegfried Sassoon in the Royal Welch Fusiliers and was killed by a sniper’s bullet in March 1916.

Graves’s collection Goliath and David was published in 1917, after the poem’s wartime setting and dedication. The later Fairies and Fusiliers (1918) also included the poem. The reference to a “spike-helmeted” Goliath identifies the biblical giant with the stereotyped German soldier and brings the ancient story into the material world of the First World War.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of “Goliath and David”?

Graves reverses the biblical victory to show how courage and faith can fail against overwhelming force. In its First World War context, David’s death becomes a memorial for young soldiers whose bravery could not protect them.

### What are the main themes in “Goliath and David”?

The main themes are the failure of heroic myth, unequal power in war, divine silence, and grief. The poem contrasts David’s courage and simple weapons with Goliath’s armor, shield, and steel.

### What is the form of “Goliath and David”?

It is a two-stanza narrative poem built mostly from rhyming couplets. Its meter varies rather than following one strict pattern, and its sound effects accelerate the battle and emphasize the final violence.

### Who was David Cuthbert Thomas?

David Cuthbert Thomas was a friend and fellow Royal Welch Fusilier of Robert Graves and Siegfried Sassoon. Graves dedicated this poem to Thomas, who was killed at Fricourt in March 1916.
