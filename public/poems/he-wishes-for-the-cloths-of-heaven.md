# He Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven analysis

He Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven — W. B. Yeats (1899)

This He Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven analysis explains how W. B. Yeats turns heavenly imagery, repetition, and a quiet final plea into a study of love's vulnerability.

## The poem

```
Had I the heavens' embroidered cloths,
Enwrought with golden and silver light,
The blue and the dim and the dark cloths
Of night and light and the half-light,
I would spread the cloths under your feet:
But I, being poor, have only my dreams;
I have spread my dreams under your feet;
Tread softly because you tread on my dreams.
```

Text from [He wishes for the Cloths of Heaven](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/He_wishes_for_the_Cloths_of_Heaven). Public domain in the US (First published in The Wind Among the Reeds in 1899, so the poem is public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The speaker imagines giving a beloved the skies themselves, pictured as embroidered cloths made from daylight, twilight, darkness, and celestial light. The wish is extravagant, but it is also carefully shaped as an offering laid beneath her feet.

The speaker then admits that he is poor and has only his dreams to give. The last two lines repeat the image of laying something under her feet, changing the imagined heavenly cloths into a fragile emotional gift and a request for care.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem treats dreams as both less tangible than wealth and more personal than any material present. The speaker cannot provide the magnificent object he imagines, so he offers the inner life that produced the vision instead.

The closing request makes love feel exposed rather than safely idealized. The beloved has power over the speaker's hopes, and the gentle imperative to tread softly acknowledges that intimacy can wound as easily as it can console.

## Themes

### Love and vulnerability

The speaker's final plea reveals the risk inside his devotion. By placing his dreams beneath the beloved's feet, he gives her access to something precious and asks her not to damage it.

### Imagination versus material poverty

The poem moves from impossible riches to the plain statement that the speaker is poor. His lack of material wealth does not end the offering; it redirects the gift toward dreams, imagination, and feeling.

### Idealization

The beloved is placed above cloths made from the heavens, while the speaker imagines himself serving her. The scale of the image shows how love can raise another person into a nearly divine figure.

## Form and meter

The poem is a single eight-line stanza, or octave. Its ABAB CDCD pattern uses identical end words as rhymes: “cloths” with “cloths,” “light” with “light,” “feet” with “feet,” and “dreams” with “dreams.”

The rhythm is loose accentual verse with roughly four strong beats per line rather than a fixed pattern of feet. The final line gathers its stresses around “Tread,” “tread,” and “dreams,” making the request sound hushed and urgent.

The repeated end words and the return from “cloths” to “dreams” make the short poem feel tightly woven. Its compact form holds a large imagined sky and a private emotional confession in the same space.

## Literary devices

### Extended metaphor

> Had I the heavens' embroidered cloths

The sky becomes fabric that can be embroidered, folded, and spread beneath the beloved. This impossible transformation gives the speaker's love a physical shape before the poem turns to the more intimate metaphor of dreams as a floor.

### Imagery

> The blue and the dim and the dark cloths

The sequence of colors and degrees of light moves through day, twilight, and night. The speaker creates a whole sky from visual details, then offers that imagined richness as a sign of devotion.

### Repetition and parallelism

> I would spread the cloths under your feet: / ... / I have spread my dreams under your feet;

The repeated sentence shape links the fantasy of heavenly cloths to the reality of dreams. The change from “would” to “have” turns a hypothetical gift into the actual offering the speaker can make.

### Polysyndeton

> The blue and the dim and the dark cloths / Of night and light and the half-light

The repeated “and” slows the description and piles one condition of the sky on another. The abundance of conjunctions suits the speaker's attempt to imagine enough beauty for the beloved.

## Historical context

Yeats published the poem in The Wind Among the Reeds in 1899. The poem first appeared under the title “Aedh Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven”; later publications used the shorter title.

Aedh is one of the archetypal figures in Yeats's early poetry. LitCharts connects the lovelorn speaker with Yeats's long, unreturned love for Maud Gonne, but the poem itself leaves the beloved unnamed.

The poem belongs to Yeats's early, dreamlike work, where mythic or visionary images give private feeling a larger setting. Here, the whole sky becomes the imagined surface on which the speaker places his hopes.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of “He Wishes for the Cloths of Heaven”?

The speaker offers his dreams to the person he loves. Because those dreams are fragile and deeply personal, he asks her to treat them gently.

### What are the main themes in the poem?

The main themes are romantic vulnerability, imagination, poverty, and idealized love. The poem contrasts the riches the speaker wishes he could give with the dreams he can actually offer.

### What is the form and rhyme scheme of the poem?

It is one eight-line stanza with an ABAB CDCD pattern. The poem uses loose four-beat accentual verse rather than a strict regular meter.

### Why does the speaker say “Tread softly”?

He asks the beloved to be careful because she is walking on his dreams. The line expresses both trust and fear: accepting his love gives her the power to hurt him.
