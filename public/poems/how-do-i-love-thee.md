# How Do I Love Thee? analysis

How Do I Love Thee? (Sonnet 43) — Elizabeth Barrett Browning (1850)

Elizabeth Barrett Browning’s “How Do I Love Thee?” turns a question about love into a measured sequence of spiritual, ethical, and everyday answers. This analysis follows how its sonnet form makes an apparently infinite devotion feel both ordered and intimate.

## The poem

```
How do I love thee? Let me count the ways.
I love thee to the depth and breadth and height
My soul can reach, when feeling out of sight
For the ends of being and ideal grace.

I love thee to the level of every day’s
Most quiet need, by sun and candle-light.
I love thee freely, as men strive for right;
I love thee purely, as they turn from praise.

I love thee with the passion put to use
In my old griefs, and with my childhood’s faith.
I love thee with a love I seemed to lose
With my lost saints. I love thee with the breath,
Smiles, tears, of all my life; and, if God choose,
I shall but love thee better after death.
```

Text from [How Do I Love Thee? — Academy of American Poets](https://poets.org/poem/how-do-i-love-thee-sonnet-43). Public domain in the US (First published in Sonnets from the Portuguese in 1850; the Academy of American Poets identifies the poem as public domain.).

## Summary

The speaker answers her opening question by naming the dimensions of her love: it reaches through the soul, ordinary daily life, moral choice, memory, and the whole range of her lived feeling.

The poem closes by extending that devotion beyond mortal life. Its catalog begins as an attempt to count love, but its spiritual scale finally suggests something that cannot be fully measured.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem presents love as both transcendent and practical. The speaker reaches toward “the ends of being and ideal grace,” then brings that love down to the quiet needs of every day, suggesting that lasting devotion is made of ordinary care as well as spiritual aspiration.

The repeated declarations also make love an act of agency. She loves “freely” and “purely,” not because an outside authority commands her to do so. Read alongside the poem’s references to childhood faith and lost saints, the beloved becomes associated with a recovered intensity of feeling, while “if God choose” keeps the promise of eternity within a framework of religious humility.

## Themes

### Boundless and eternal love

The speaker measures love through “depth and breadth and height,” then imagines loving “better after death.” Spatial and temporal language steadily pushes devotion beyond ordinary limits.

### Love in daily life

The phrase “every day’s / Most quiet need” grounds the poem’s grand spiritual claims in routine companionship. Love is not only an overwhelming feeling; it is a presence sustained through daylight, candlelight, breath, smiles, and tears.

### Choice, freedom, and moral feeling

The speaker describes love as something chosen “freely” and “purely.” By comparing it with striving for right and turning from praise, she gives private affection an ethical dimension rather than treating it as mere impulse.

### Romantic and religious devotion

References to the soul, ideal grace, childhood faith, lost saints, and God place romantic love beside religious experience. The poem does not simply replace faith with romance; it imagines earthly love as capable of restoring and extending spiritual feeling.

## Form and meter

This is a Petrarchan, or Italian, sonnet: fourteen lines divided into an octave and a sestet. The octave follows an ABBA ABBA rhyme pattern, while the sestet uses CDCDCD.

The poem is written primarily in iambic pentameter, with five rising beats per line. The regular pulse supports the speaker’s promise to “count the ways,” while punctuation, enjambment, and repeated openings keep the voice emotionally expansive.

The octave develops the scale and moral character of love. The sestet turns toward remembered grief and faith before the final conditional vision of love after death, functioning as a deepening rather than a sharp argumentative reversal.

## Literary devices

### Anaphora

> I love thee

The repeated opening “I love thee” creates the poem’s incantatory structure. Each repetition adds another register of devotion while making the list feel too abundant to finish.

### Measurement imagery

> to the depth and breadth and height

The speaker borrows the language of quantity and spatial measurement to answer “How” she loves. Because the soul’s reach has no stated boundary, the image turns counting into an encounter with the infinite.

### Simile

> I love thee freely, as men strive for right

The comparisons to striving for right and turning from praise connect love with ethical action. They characterize devotion as voluntary, disciplined, and independent of vanity.

### Antithesis and paradox

> I love thee with a love I seemed to lose

The speaker describes a love that was apparently lost through the language of recovery. The paradox lets romantic love carry the force of earlier grief and childhood faith without reducing either experience to a simple replacement.

## Historical context

The sonnet appeared in Sonnets from the Portuguese in 1850. The collection contains forty-four sonnets written during Elizabeth Barrett Browning’s courtship with Robert Browning; its misleading title presented the poems as if they were translations.

The sequence was written before the Brownings’ secret marriage in 1846 and was kept in a notebook for several years. The Poetry Foundation describes the sonnets as expressing Barrett Browning’s sense that her life had been transformed by love after illness, grief, and confinement.

The poem itself leaves the speaker and beloved unnamed, so the traditional biographical reading is useful context rather than a requirement. Its language also supports a broader reading of love as a chosen, spiritual, and enduring human attachment.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of “How Do I Love Thee?”

The speaker describes love as spiritual, ethical, ordinary, and enduring. She begins by counting its forms but ends by suggesting that true devotion reaches beyond measurement and death.

### What are the main themes in Sonnet 43?

The main themes are boundless love, everyday devotion, freedom of choice, spiritual faith, memory, and the hope that love continues after death.

### What form and meter does the poem use?

It is a Petrarchan sonnet with an ABBA ABBA CDCDCD rhyme scheme, usually written in iambic pentameter. The octave develops the idea of love and the sestet intensifies it through memory, faith, and the afterlife.

### Who is the speaker addressing in Sonnet 43?

The poem does not name the beloved. It is traditionally read as Elizabeth Barrett Browning’s address to Robert Browning, but the unnamed “thee” also allows the poem to speak more broadly about intimate love.
