# I, Too, Sing America analysis

I, Too, Sing America — Langston Hughes (1926)

Langston Hughes’s “I, Too” answers an older American song with a voice that segregation tries to hide. This analysis follows the poem from the kitchen to the table, where the speaker’s claim to America becomes impossible to dismiss.

## The poem

```
I, too, sing America.

I am the darker brother.
They send me to eat in the kitchen
When company comes,
But I laugh,
And eat well,
And grow strong.

Tomorrow,
I’ll be at the table
When company comes.
Nobody’ll dare
Say to me,
“Eat in the kitchen,”
Then.

Besides,
They’ll see how beautiful I am
And be ashamed—

I, too, am America.
```

Text from [I, Too](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/47558/i-too). Public domain in the US (First published in The Weary Blues in 1926. The poem is included here as an editorial analysis module, with the text source and copyright credit provided by the Poetry Foundation.).

## Summary

The speaker names himself as America’s “darker brother,” then describes being sent away from the shared meal when company arrives. The kitchen is a place of exclusion, but it also becomes the place where he eats, laughs, and grows strong.

The poem moves from “They send me” to “Tomorrow, / I’ll be at the table.” Its ending turns the first line into a firmer claim: the speaker does not merely sing America. He is America.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem treats belonging as a fact that racism can deny socially but cannot erase. “Too” answers the claim that America has one authorized voice, while the repeated first-person pronoun keeps the excluded speaker in view.

The kitchen and table form the poem’s central contrast. The kitchen represents enforced separation, but the speaker’s confidence prevents it from becoming a permanent definition of his place. “Tomorrow” is a promise of racial equality and a warning to those who think exclusion can last.

The final word, “America,” changes the scale of the poem. The speaker is not asking to borrow someone else’s national identity. He claims that Black life and labor already belong inside the country’s definition of itself.

## Themes

### American belonging

The speaker’s repeated “I, too” insists that Black identity is part of the national voice. The poem makes belonging declarative rather than conditional.

### Segregation and equality

The kitchen is a domestic image for racial segregation. The anticipated move to the table imagines equality as shared presence, not merely private acceptance.

### Strength and self-respect

The speaker laughs, eats well, and grows strong while excluded. Those plain verbs give the poem its confidence: the people pushed out of sight continue to live and develop power.

## Form and meter

The poem uses free verse in eighteen short lines arranged as uneven units rather than a fixed stanza pattern. Its conversational syntax and pauses make the poem sound close to speech.

Repetition supplies much of the structure. “When company comes” returns as a pressure point, while “I, too” opens and closes the poem with a change from singing America to being America.

The short lines slow the reader around individual words such as “Tomorrow,” “Besides,” and “Then.” These pauses let the speaker control the pace of his argument, even when the people in the poem try to control his place.

## Literary devices

### Allusion

> I, too, sing America.

The opening echoes Walt Whitman’s “I Hear America Singing.” Hughes keeps Whitman’s democratic song but answers its limited picture of who gets to sing by placing a Black speaker at the center.

### Extended metaphor

> They send me to eat in the kitchen

The household becomes a picture of the nation. Sending the speaker to the kitchen makes racial exclusion visible as a physical arrangement, and the table becomes the image of equal citizenship.

### Repetition

> When company comes ... When company comes.

The repeated phrase shows that exclusion is treated as routine. When it returns after “I’ll be at the table,” the same words now frame a changed social order.

### Future tense

> Tomorrow, / I’ll be at the table

The shift from present exclusion to future participation gives the poem its forward motion. “Tomorrow” sounds patient, but “Nobody’ll dare” makes the prediction unmistakably forceful.

## Historical context

Hughes published “I, Too” in The Weary Blues in 1926, during the Harlem Renaissance. The Poetry Foundation describes Hughes as a central figure in that flowering of Black intellectual, literary, and artistic life, and places the poem among the work of his first book.

The poem replies to Walt Whitman’s “I Hear America Singing,” whose catalog of American workers presents a national chorus. Hughes keeps Whitman’s democratic language and adds the voice that the earlier poem leaves out. The Poetry Foundation calls this a reckoning with Hughes’s literary forebears.

The kitchen and table draw on the everyday logic of segregation. The image is domestic rather than abstract: one person is made invisible when guests arrive, then predicts a day when the same household must recognize his beauty and equality.

Hughes later described “I, Too” as directly concerned with the race problem. That context matters because the poem’s optimism is not vague uplift. Its “tomorrow” answers a specific system of exclusion with a specific demand for a seat at the table.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of “I, Too, Sing America”?

The poem asserts that Black Americans belong fully to the nation, even when racism tries to exclude them. The speaker predicts that he will move from the kitchen to the table and be recognized as America.

### How does “I, Too” respond to Walt Whitman?

Hughes echoes Whitman’s “I Hear America Singing,” but adds the voice of the “darker brother.” He keeps the idea of America as a shared song while showing that the earlier vision did not make Black experience visible.

### What does the kitchen symbolize in the poem?

The kitchen symbolizes segregation and enforced invisibility. It is also where the speaker eats well and grows strong, so the image carries both the injury of exclusion and the strength that exclusion cannot contain.

### Why does the speaker say “I, too, am America”?

The final line makes belonging direct and undeniable. The speaker is not outside the nation waiting for permission; his Black life is already part of what America is.
