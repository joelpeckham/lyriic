# If— analysis

If— — Rudyard Kipling (1910)

Rudyard Kipling’s If— presents maturity as a long test of self-command. Its famous advice about patience, courage, and balance remains memorable, but the poem also carries the assumptions of late-Victorian masculinity and empire that shaped its origins.

## The poem

```
If you can keep your head when all about you
Are losing theirs and blaming it on you,
If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you,
But make allowance for their doubting too;
If you can wait and not be tired by waiting,
Or being lied about, don’t deal in lies,
Or being hated, don’t give way to hating,
And yet don’t look too good, nor talk too wise:

If you can dream—and not make dreams your master;
If you can think—and not make thoughts your aim;
If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster
And treat those two impostors just the same;
If you can bear to hear the truth you’ve spoken
Twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools,
Or watch the things you gave your life to, broken,
And stoop and build ’em up with worn-out tools:

If you can make one heap of all your winnings
And risk it on one turn of pitch-and-toss,
And lose, and start again at your beginnings
And never breathe a word about your loss;
If you can force your heart and nerve and sinew
To serve your turn long after they are gone,
And so hold on when there is nothing in you
Except the Will which says to them: ‘Hold on!’

If you can talk with crowds and keep your virtue,
Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch,
If neither foes nor loving friends can hurt you,
If all men count with you, but none too much;
If you can fill the unforgiving minute
With sixty seconds’ worth of distance run,
Yours is the Earth and everything that’s in it,
And—which is more—you’ll be a Man, my son!
```

Text from [If— — Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/46473/if---). Public domain in the US (First published in 1910 in Kipling’s Rewards and Fairies, If— is in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The poem addresses a son through a chain of conditional tests: remain calm under accusation, trust yourself without becoming arrogant, endure delay, resist hatred, rebuild after loss, and keep your balance around both crowds and kings.

The final promise—owning “the Earth”—is made conditional on disciplined conduct. The closing “Man” gives the advice its original gendered frame, while modern readers may separate the poem’s useful ethics from that limitation.

## Meaning and interpretation

If— defines stoic virtue as active self-government rather than emotional emptiness. The speaker does not ask the reader to avoid pain, failure, lies, or hatred; he asks for a chosen response to them.

The poem repeatedly insists on proportion. Dreams must not become masters, triumph and disaster must be treated alike, and people should count without becoming everything. Its ideal is a self that can participate fully without being possessed by outcomes.

The conditions accumulate until the promise at the end feels both inspiring and nearly impossible. The poem’s “counsels of perfection” can motivate resilience, but they can also make vulnerability, dependence, and ordinary failure look like moral defects.

## Themes

### Stoic virtue

Keeping one’s head, refusing hatred, and treating triumph and disaster alike make composure the poem’s central virtue. This is not passive surrender: the reader must wait, rebuild, risk, and keep acting.

### Resilience after failure

The third stanza imagines total loss, then asks the reader to begin again without self-pity. Its worn-out tools and empty body make persistence physical and costly rather than merely motivational.

### Balance and self-command

The poem pairs opposites—dream and thought, crowds and kings, foes and friends, triumph and disaster. Maturity means moving between extremes without allowing any one force to rule the self.

### Masculinity and power

The final claim that the reader will be “a Man” places moral achievement inside a specifically male, paternal ideal. The poem’s universal afterlife is therefore in tension with its historical assumptions about gender and authority.

## Form and meter

If— consists of four eight-line stanzas. Each stanza is one long conditional sentence, delaying its main conclusion until the final two lines. The repeated “If you can” creates anaphora and a cumulative, speech-like momentum.

The poem uses predominantly iambic movement with frequent substitutions and varied line lengths rather than a mechanically uniform meter. Its alternating rhyme is generally ABABCDCD within each stanza, with punctuation and parallel syntax helping organize the long argument.

The direct address, semicolons, and repeated clauses make the poem sound like spoken instruction. The regular returns of “If” turn ethical advice into a test, while the final “And—which is more—” supplies the delayed release.

## Literary devices

### Anaphora and conditional syntax

> If you can keep your head… / If you can trust yourself… / If you can wait…

The repeated opening clause makes every virtue a condition of the final reward. It also keeps the father’s voice in control of the sentence for almost the entire poem.

### Personification

> Triumph and Disaster / And treat those two impostors just the same;

Abstract outcomes become deceptive performers or impostors. Calling them impostors reduces their authority and asks the reader not to confuse circumstance with identity.

### Antithesis and parallelism

> Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch,

Paired opposites test whether the speaker can remain recognizably himself in different social settings. The balanced syntax models the equilibrium it recommends.

### Metaphor

> make one heap of all your winnings / And risk it on one turn of pitch-and-toss

Life becomes a wager in which accumulated gains may be risked and lost. The image celebrates nerve and renewal, but it also exposes the poem’s willingness to turn economic risk into a moral trial.

## Historical context

Kipling conceived If— around 1895 and published it in 1910 in Rewards and Fairies, where it follows the story “Brother Square-Toes.” The poem is addressed as paternal advice, but its immediate historical inspiration was the composure Kipling associated with Leander Starr Jameson after the failed 1895 Jameson Raid in South Africa.

That origin matters because the Jameson Raid was part of the conflict surrounding British expansion in the Transvaal and helped intensify tensions that led toward the Second Boer War. The poem’s endurance and self-command can therefore be read alongside an imperial culture that praised action, discipline, and masculine authority.

A modern reading should hold two facts together. The poem offers portable advice about honesty, patience, and rebuilding after loss; it also ends by equating moral completion with becoming “a Man” and promises possession of “the Earth,” language that can echo the gender hierarchy and colonial confidence of its period.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of If—?

The poem presents maturity as disciplined self-command: remain honest, patient, resilient, and balanced when circumstances turn against you. Its ideals are powerful, but the impossible-sounding list also invites criticism of its narrow standards.

### What inspired Rudyard Kipling to write If—?

Kipling was inspired in part by Leander Starr Jameson and the failed Jameson Raid of 1895. He later shaped that historical admiration into paternal advice, and the poem was published in Rewards and Fairies in 1910.

### Is If— a stoic poem?

It is a modern literary expression of stoic self-control, especially in its advice to treat triumph and disaster alike. It is not a direct philosophical summary of ancient Stoicism, and its Victorian imperial and masculine assumptions deserve examination.

### What meter does If— use?

If— uses predominantly iambic movement with substitutions and varied line lengths. Its four eight-line stanzas, alternating rhyme, anaphora, and long conditional sentence are more reliable formal guides than forcing every line into one strict metrical label.
