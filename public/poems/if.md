# If— analysis

If— — Rudyard Kipling (1910)

This analysis examines Kipling’s advice about self-command alongside its Victorian gender and imperial assumptions.

Full text from [If— — full poem](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/46473/if---). Public domain in the US (First published in Rewards and Fairies in 1910, before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The poem addresses a son through conditional tests: remain calm, resist hatred, rebuild after loss, and keep balance around crowds and kings.[1]

```
If you can keep your head when all about you
Are losing theirs and blaming it on you,
If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you,
```

The final promise of owning the Earth is conditional on disciplined conduct, but “a Man” reveals the poem’s original gendered frame.[2]

## Meaning and interpretation

If— defines maturity as active self-government rather than emotional emptiness. It asks readers to choose responses to pain, failure, lies, and hatred.[2]

```
If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster
And treat those two impostors just the same;
```

The accumulating conditions inspire resilience but can also make vulnerability and ordinary failure look like moral defects.[2]

## Themes

### Stoic virtue

Composure, integrity, and proportion are treated as central virtues.

### Resilience

The speaker asks the son to rebuild from total loss without self-pity.

### Balance

Opposites—dream and thought, crowds and kings, triumph and disaster—test equilibrium.

### Masculinity and power

The ending makes moral completion specifically male and promises possession of the Earth.[2]

## Form and meter

Four eight-line stanzas are each one long conditional sentence; repeated “If you can” creates anaphora and cumulative momentum.[4]

Predominantly iambic movement, alternating rhyme, semicolons, and parallel clauses make the poem sound like spoken instruction.[4]

## Literary devices

### Anaphora

```
If you can dream—and not make dreams your master;
If you can think—and not make thoughts your aim;
```

The repeated conditional makes every virtue a test of the final reward.

### Personification

```
And treat those two impostors just the same;
```

Triumph and Disaster become deceptive figures whose authority can be resisted.

### Antithesis

```
Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch,
```

Balanced opposites model the equilibrium the poem recommends.

## Historical context

Kipling published the poem in Rewards and Fairies in 1910; the Kipling Society records his statement that it was drawn from Leander Starr Jameson’s character.[3]

Austin Allen reads the poem as Victorian didactic rhetoric whose confidence contains an anxiety about whether its conditions can ever be met.[2]

## Common questions

### What is If— about?

It presents maturity as patient, honest, resilient, balanced self-command.

### What inspired the poem?

Kipling associated it with Leander Starr Jameson and published it in Rewards and Fairies in 1910.

### What form does it use?

Four eight-line stanzas built from anaphora, conditional syntax, alternating rhyme, and predominantly iambic movement.
