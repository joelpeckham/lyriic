# In Praise of Solid People analysis

In Praise of Solid People — C. S. Lewis (1919)

This analysis of C. S. Lewis’s In Praise of Solid People explains why the speaker envies ordinary domestic life, and how the poem’s regular form contains his restless imagination.

## The poem

```
Thank God that there are solid folk
Who water flowers and roll the lawn,
And sit an sew and talk and smoke,
And snore all through the summer dawn.

Who pass untroubled nights and days
Full-fed and sleepily content,
Rejoicing in each other's praise,
Respectable and innocent.

Who feel the things that all men feel,
And think in well-worn grooves of thought,
Whose honest spirits never reel
Before man's mystery, overwrought.

Yet not unfaithful nor unkind,
with work-day virtues surely staid,
Theirs is the sane and humble mind,
And dull affections undismayed.

O happy people! I have seen
No verse yet written in your praise,
And, truth to tell, the time has been
I would have scorned your easy ways.

But now thro' weariness and strife
I learn your worthiness indeed,
The world is better for such life
As stout suburban people lead.

Too often have I sat alone
When the wet night falls heavily,
And fretting winds around me moan,
And homeless longing vexes me

For lore that I shall never know,
And visions none can hope to see,
Till brooding works upon me so
A childish fear steals over me.

I look around the empty room,
The clock still ticking in its place,
And all else silent as the tomb,
Till suddenly, I think, a face

Grows from the darkness just beside.
I turn, and lo! it fades away,
And soon another phantom tide
Of shifting dreams begins to play,

And dusky galleys past me sail,
Full freighted on a faerie sea;
I hear the silken merchants hail
Across the ringing waves to me

—Then suddenly, again, the room,
Familiar books about me piled,
And I alone amid the gloom,
By one more mocking dream beguiled.

And still no neared to the Light,
And still no further from myself,
Alone and lost in clinging night—
(The clock's still ticking on the shelf).

Then do I envy solid folk
Who sit of evenings by the fire,
After their work and doze and smoke,
And are not fretted by desire.
```

Text from [Spirits in Bondage at Project Gutenberg](https://gutenberg.org/files/2003/2003-h/2003-h.htm). Public domain in the US (First published in 1919 in Spirits in Bondage, this poem is in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The speaker begins by thanking God for “solid folk”: ordinary people whose domestic routines, mutual affection, and practical virtues give them an untroubled stability. He admits that he once scorned their supposedly easy lives.

After describing lonely nights filled with storms, phantom faces, and impossible visions, the speaker returns to the same grounded people. His praise is therefore also a confession: imaginative and spiritual longing has left him exhausted, while ordinary contentment now looks like a form of wisdom.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem’s meaning turns on a reversal of values. The speaker once treated conventional life as intellectually narrow, but “weariness and strife” have taught him that a sane, humble mind and dependable affection can be genuine goods. The poem does not claim that curiosity is worthless; it shows the emotional cost of desire that has no object it can reach.

The final image of the ticking clock makes the speaker’s visions feel temporary and unproductive. He is left “no neared to the Light” and “no further from myself,” so the solid people represent not ignorance alone but a peace he cannot manufacture through solitary speculation.

## Themes

### Contentment versus restless desire

The solid people are “full-fed and sleepily content,” while the speaker is “fretted by desire.” Their ordinary pleasures become attractive because his search for lore and visions produces longing without resolution.

### The dignity of ordinary life

Watering flowers, rolling the lawn, working, smoking, and sitting by the fire are presented as stabilizing practices rather than trivial routines. The speaker’s revised judgment gives domestic life moral and communal value.

### Loneliness and spiritual uncertainty

The empty room, storm, phantom face, and distance from “the Light” turn private isolation into a spiritual crisis. The speaker is surrounded by books and visions but remains alone with himself.

## Form and meter

The poem has thirteen quatrains, with predominantly alternating ABAB rhyme and a generally iambic-tetrameter pulse. Its repeated four-line units create the regularity that the speaker admires in the lives he describes.

The rhythm is flexible rather than mechanically strict: natural speech, substitutions, and longer-feeling lines allow the speaker’s unease to surface inside the orderly stanza pattern. The return to “solid folk” at the close gives the poem a circular, refrain-like structure.

## Literary devices

### Contrast

> Who pass untroubled nights and days / Full-fed and sleepily content,

The settled routines of the solid people are set against the speaker’s sleepless brooding and unfulfilled desire, making the poem’s admiration depend on opposition.

### Pathetic fallacy

> When the wet night falls heavily, / And fretting winds around me moan,

The weather seems to share the speaker’s agitation. The heavy night and moaning wind externalize an emotional state that later becomes hallucinated or dreamlike.

### Recurrent clock imagery

> (The clock's still ticking on the shelf).

The clock anchors the speaker in measurable domestic time whenever imagination carries him into phantoms and faerie seas. Its repetition emphasizes that the visions change while his isolation remains.

### Mythic and fantastical imagery

> And dusky galleys past me sail, / Full freighted on a faerie sea;

The imagined ships and merchants enlarge private longing into an uncanny voyage. Their beauty is also “mocking,” because the speaker wakes from them without gaining knowledge or spiritual nearness.

## Historical context

Spirits in Bondage was Lewis’s first book, published in 1919 under the pseudonym Clive Hamilton. Project Gutenberg’s historical note says most of the poems were written between 1915 and 1918, during Lewis’s education, military training, and service in the trenches of World War I.

The poem belongs to the collection’s middle section, “Hesitation.” That placement fits its movement away from outright complaint toward an uncertain admiration for stability, without giving the speaker a final theological resolution. Lewis was twenty when the collection appeared and had not yet become the Christian writer of his later career.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of In Praise of Solid People?

The speaker learns to value ordinary, stable domestic life after finding that intense intellectual and imaginative longing has made him lonely and exhausted.

### What are the main themes in In Praise of Solid People?

The main themes are contentment versus restless desire, the dignity of ordinary life, loneliness, and spiritual uncertainty.

### What form and meter does the poem use?

It uses thirteen quatrains with mostly alternating ABAB rhyme and a generally iambic-tetrameter rhythm, though the meter flexes with the speaker’s troubled voice.
