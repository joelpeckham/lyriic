# Jabberwocky analysis

Jabberwocky — Lewis Carroll (1871)

This Jabberwocky analysis explains the poem’s meaning, themes, form, and literary devices.

Full text from [Jabberwocky](https://gutenberg.org/files/12/12-h/12-h.htm). Public domain in the US (First published in 1871 or earlier, placing this text in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

A father warns his son about strange creatures; the son hunts and kills the Jabberwock, then returns in triumph. The repeated opening stanza frames the adventure.

```
’Twas brillig, and the slithy toves
Did gyre and gimble in the wabe:
All mimsy were the borogoves,
```

The poem’s structure makes its central tension memorable.[1]

## Meaning and interpretation

Carroll keeps English syntax, rhyme, and narrative sequence while replacing much vocabulary with inventions. Readers infer action and mood through grammar, sound, and context.[1]

```
One, two! One, two! And through and through
The vorpal blade went snicker-snack!
He left it dead, and with its head
```

Its images turn an abstract concern into a scene readers can hear and see.[2]

## Themes

### Meaning through sound and context

The poem returns to meaning through sound and context through recurring images and shifts in voice.[1]

### Playful heroic adventure

```
One, two! One, two! And through and through
The vorpal blade went snicker-snack!
He left it dead, and with its head
```

This theme is developed through the poem’s contrast between image and argument.[2]

## Form and meter

Seven quatrains broadly follow ballad meter and an ABAB rhyme pattern. The familiar structure anchors invented words and lets the poem sound like a story before every word is understood.[2]

```
’Twas brillig, and the slithy toves
Did gyre and gimble in the wabe:
All mimsy were the borogoves,
```

## Literary devices

### Portmanteau words

```
’Twas brillig, and the slithy toves
Did gyre and gimble in the wabe:
All mimsy were the borogoves,
```

The device gives the scene an emotional or rhetorical force beyond literal description.

### Onomatopoeia

```
One, two! One, two! And through and through
The vorpal blade went snicker-snack!
He left it dead, and with its head
```

The repeated image or sound helps connect the local detail to the poem’s larger meaning.

## Historical context

“Jabberwocky” appeared in Through the Looking-Glass in 1871. Humpty Dumpty later explains some portmanteaus, but the poem’s energy depends on meanings that remain partly unstable.[4]

The poem’s later reception also shapes how readers understand its central images.[2]
