# Love and Friendship analysis

Love and Friendship — Emily Brontë (1846)

This Love and Friendship analysis explains the poem’s meaning, themes, form, and literary devices.

Full text from [Love and Friendship](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Complete_Poems_of_Emily_Bront%C3%AB/Love_and_Friendship). Public domain in the US (First published in 1846 or earlier, placing this text in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

Love is compared with a fragrant wild rose-briar; friendship is compared with evergreen holly. Winter tests the relationships and gives the holly the advantage.

```
Love is like the wild rose-briar; Friendship like the holly-tree.
The holly is dark when the rose-briar blooms,
But which will bloom most constantly?
```

The poem’s structure makes its central tension memorable.[1]

## Meaning and interpretation

The plants make an abstract distinction visible: romance may be beautiful and intense, while friendship is less showy but more dependable through hardship, aging, and disappointment.[1]

```
Then, scorn the silly rose-wreath now,
And deck thee with the holly’s sheen,
That, when December blights thy brow,
```

Its images turn an abstract concern into a scene readers can hear and see.[2]

## Themes

### Constancy in friendship

The poem returns to constancy in friendship through recurring images and shifts in voice.[1]

### Beauty versus endurance

```
Then, scorn the silly rose-wreath now,
And deck thee with the holly’s sheen,
That, when December blights thy brow,
```

This theme is developed through the poem’s contrast between image and argument.[2]

## Form and meter

Three quatrains use a songlike, predominantly iambic movement and alternating rhyme. The final stanza turns the comparison into direct advice.[2]

```
Love is like the wild rose-briar; Friendship like the holly-tree.
The holly is dark when the rose-briar blooms,
But which will bloom most constantly?
```

## Literary devices

### Extended metaphor

```
Love is like the wild rose-briar; Friendship like the holly-tree.
The holly is dark when the rose-briar blooms,
But which will bloom most constantly?
```

The device gives the scene an emotional or rhetorical force beyond literal description.

### Seasonal imagery

```

And deck thee with the holly’s sheen,
That, when December blights thy brow,
```

The repeated image or sound helps connect the local detail to the poem’s larger meaning.

## Historical context

The poem appeared in Poems by Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell in 1846. Emily Brontë published under the name Ellis Bell alongside her sisters.[4]

The poem’s later reception also shapes how readers understand its central images.[2]
