# Loveliest of Trees analysis

Loveliest of Trees — A. E. Housman (1896)

This Loveliest of Trees analysis explains the poem’s meaning, themes, form, and literary devices.

Full text from [Loveliest of Trees](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Shropshire_Lad). Public domain in the US (First published in 1896 or earlier, placing this text in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

A cherry tree in Easter bloom prompts the speaker to calculate the springs remaining in a seventy-year life. Mortality leads him to walk into the woods and attend to beauty.

```
Loveliest of trees, the cherry now
Is hung with bloom along the bough,
And stands about the woodland ride
```

The poem’s structure makes its central tension memorable.[1]

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem joins carpe diem to memento mori. The finite number of future springs makes beauty more urgent, not less valuable; reflection becomes a decision to go and look.[1]

```
And since to look at things in bloom
Fifty springs are little room,
About the woodlands I will go
```

Its images turn an abstract concern into a scene readers can hear and see.[2]

## Themes

### Mortality and time

The poem returns to mortality and time through recurring images and shifts in voice.[1]

### Transience and beauty

```
And since to look at things in bloom
Fifty springs are little room,
About the woodlands I will go
```

This theme is developed through the poem’s contrast between image and argument.[2]

## Form and meter

Three quatrains are arranged as six rhyming couplets, AABB CCDD EEFF, in predominantly iambic tetrameter. The walking rhythm supports the final movement into the woods.[2]

```
Loveliest of trees, the cherry now
Is hung with bloom along the bough,
And stands about the woodland ride
```

## Historical context

The poem is the second lyric in A Shropshire Lad, published in 1896. “Threescore years and ten” echoes Psalm 90:10, while Eastertide adds renewal to the mortality meditation.[4]

The poem’s later reception also shapes how readers understand its central images.[2]
