# Loveliest of Trees analysis

Loveliest of Trees — A. E. Housman (1896)

This analysis explains how A. E. Housman turns a cherry tree in spring into a meditation on mortality, beauty, and the value of using time well.

## The poem

```
Loveliest of trees, the cherry now
Is hung with bloom along the bough,
And stands about the woodland ride
Wearing white for Eastertide.

Now, of my threescore years and ten,
Twenty will not come again,
And take from seventy springs a score,
It only leaves me fifty more.

And since to look at things in bloom
Fifty springs are little room,
About the woodlands I will go
To see the cherry hung with snow.
```

Text from [A Shropshire Lad](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Shropshire_Lad). Public domain in the US (The poem was first published in A Shropshire Lad (1896), placing the original English text well before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The speaker sees a cherry tree covered in white blossom and immediately connects its brief spring display with the limited span of human life. The tree is beautiful because it is in bloom now, but that “now” will not last.

After calculating that only fifty of his imagined seventy years remain, the speaker decides to walk through the woods and look at the cherry trees. Mortality produces action rather than despair: the awareness that beauty passes makes attention more urgent.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem’s carpe diem meaning is simple but not shallow: a finite life should be spent noticing and experiencing what is beautiful while it is available. The final decision to go into the woods turns reflection into a practical response.

The speaker’s arithmetic makes time feel countable. By treating years as “springs,” Housman links human aging to a recurring natural season, while the phrase “little room” suggests that even fifty future opportunities are insufficient for all the beauty one might want to see.

The poem balances celebration with a memento mori. Easter imagery and white blossom suggest renewal, but the remembered twenty years and the future loss of the flowers keep death inside the scene. The result is stoic attention: impermanence is a reason to value the present.

## Themes

### Mortality and time

The speaker measures a conventional seventy-year life against the twenty years already gone. The calculation gives an abstract future a concrete limit and makes each remaining spring feel precious.

### Transience and beauty

Cherry blossom is both lovely and temporary. Its short season becomes a natural image for youth, pleasure, and life itself: what passes quickly deserves close attention.

### Carpe diem

The poem does not end with a maxim but with movement. After thinking about the shortage of time, the speaker goes to see the trees, showing how awareness can become deliberate enjoyment.

### Renewal and mortality

The Easter setting places the white blossoms within a season associated with rebirth. That suggestion of renewal is held in tension with the speaker’s knowledge that individual life and individual blooms will end.

## Form and meter

The poem has three quatrains, or twelve lines, organized as six rhyming couplets. The end-rhyme scheme is AABB CCDD EEFF: now/bough, ride/Eastertide, ten/again, score/more, bloom/room, and go/snow.

The prevailing meter is iambic tetrameter, giving the poem four beats per line and a steady, walking-like movement. The opening “Loveliest” begins with a strong three-syllable substitution rather than a regular iamb, creating an immediate lift in the rhythm.

The regular rhyme and meter make the mortality meditation sound bright and memorable instead of solemnly abstract. The form’s forward motion supports the final image of the speaker going out to see the trees.

## Literary devices

### Personification

> Wearing white for Eastertide.

The cherry tree is imagined as a person dressed for a religious festival. This makes the natural scene feel ceremonial and connects seasonal bloom with purity and renewal.

### Metaphor

> To see the cherry hung with snow.

The white blossoms are compared to snow. The image captures their color and softness while also hinting at coldness, transience, and the visual return of winter.

### Alliteration

> Is hung with bloom along the bough

The repeated b sounds give the line a light musical texture that echoes the abundance of flowers on the branch. Similar w sounds in “woodland,” “Wearing,” and “white” soften the scene.

### Enjambment

> Loveliest of trees, the cherry now / Is hung with bloom along the bough

The sentence runs across the first couplet, delaying the full description of the tree. The word “now” receives emphasis, reinforcing the poem’s focus on the present moment.

## Historical context

Housman first published A Shropshire Lad in 1896, with this poem appearing as the collection’s second poem under the Roman numeral II rather than under a separate title. The collection contains 63 poems and repeatedly returns to youth, death, English seasons, and the brevity of happiness.

The phrase “threescore years and ten” echoes Psalm 90:10, which gives the speaker’s calculation a biblical and proverbial resonance. “Eastertide” similarly places the blossom in a Christian season of resurrection and renewal, even as the poem remains focused on the finite human life in front of it.

Although Housman was born in Worcestershire rather than Shropshire, the collection’s imagined Shropshire setting became central to its identity. The poem’s seasonal landscape and plain song-like form helped make it one of the best-known lyrics from A Shropshire Lad.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of “Loveliest of Trees”?

The poem says that life and natural beauty are temporary, so we should pay attention to and enjoy them while we can. The speaker responds to mortality by going to see the cherry trees in bloom.

### What are the main themes in “Loveliest of Trees”?

The main themes are mortality, the passage of time, the fleeting beauty of nature, carpe diem, and the tension between seasonal renewal and human death.

### What is the form and meter of the poem?

It consists of three quatrains in rhyming couplets, with an AABB CCDD EEFF rhyme scheme and predominantly iambic tetrameter.

### What does the cherry tree symbolize?

The cherry tree symbolizes the beauty of the natural world and the freshness of youth, but its brief blossom also represents the impermanence of pleasure and life.
