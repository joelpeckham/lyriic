# My Heart and I analysis

My Heart and I — Elizabeth Barrett Browning (1862)

This My Heart and I analysis explains the poem’s meaning, themes, form, and literary devices.

Full text from [My Heart and I](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/43730/my-heart-and-i). Public domain in the US (First published in 1862 or earlier, placing this text in the public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

A speaker sits beside a grave and addresses her heart as a companion in exhaustion. Memories of Ralph and repeated refrains turn bereavement into a crisis of purpose.

```
ENOUGH ! we’re tired, my heart and I.
We sit beside the headstone thus,
And wish that name were carved for us.
```

The poem’s structure makes its central tension memorable.[1]

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem presents grief as exhaustion and loss of usefulness. Its final “well enough” is diminished consolation: past love mattered, even if it cannot restore the present.[1]

```
So tired, so tired, my heart and I !
Though now none takes me on his arm
To fold me close and kiss me warm
```

Its images turn an abstract concern into a scene readers can hear and see.[2]

## Themes

The poem returns to grief and exhaustion through recurring images and shifts in voice.[1]

## Form and meter

Seven septets use a recurring “my heart and I” refrain and predominantly iambic tetrameter. Repetition makes weariness cumulative while variations register distress.[2]

```
ENOUGH ! we’re tired, my heart and I.
We sit beside the headstone thus,
And wish that name were carved for us.
```

## Historical context

The poem was probably written around 1855, sent to Marguerite Power in 1857, and published posthumously in Last Poems in 1862. The EBB Archive identifies it as a dramatic monologue adapting ballad repetition.[4]

The poem’s later reception also shapes how readers understand its central images.[2]
