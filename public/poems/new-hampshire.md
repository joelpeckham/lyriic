# New Hampshire analysis

New Hampshire — Robert Frost (1923)

This New Hampshire analysis explains how Robert Frost turns a long, conversational defense of rural New England into a study of commerce, belonging, art, and dissatisfaction.

## The poem

```
I met a lady from the South who said
(You won’t believe she said it, but she said it):
“None of my family ever worked, or had
A thing to sell.” I don’t suppose the work
Much matters. You may work for all of me.
I’ve seen the time I’ve had to work myself.
The having anything to sell is what
Is the disgrace in man or state or nation.

I met a traveller from Arkansas
Who boasted of his state as beautiful
For diamonds and apples. “Diamonds
And apples in commercial quantities?”
I asked him, on my guard. “Oh yes,” he answered,
Off his. The time was evening in the Pullman.
“I see the porter’s made your bed,” I told him.

I met a Californian who would
Talk California—a state so blessed,
He said, in climate none had ever died there
A natural death, and Vigilance Committees
Had had to organize to stock the graveyards
And vindicate the state’s humanity.
“Just the way Steffanson runs on,” I murmured,
“About the British Arctic. That’s what comes
Of being in the market with a climate.”

I met a poet from another state,
A zealot full of fluid inspiration,
Who in the name of fluid inspiration,
But in the best style of bad salesmanship,
Angrily tried to make me write a protest
(In verse I think) against the Volstead Act.
He didn’t even offer me a drink
Until I asked for one to steady him.
This is called having an idea to sell.

It never could have happened in New Hampshire.

Just specimens is all New Hampshire has,
One each of everything as in a show-case
Which naturally she doesn’t care to sell.

She had one President (pronounce him Purse,
And make the most of it for better or worse.
He’s your one chance to score against the state).
She had one Daniel Webster. He was all
The Daniel Webster ever was or shall be.
She had the Dartmouth needed to produce him.

She has a touch of gold. New Hampshire gold—
You may have heard of it. I had a farm
Offered me not long since up Berlin way
With a mine on it that was worked for gold;
But not gold in commercial quantities.
Just enough gold to make the engagement rings
And marriage rings of those who owned the farm.
What gold more innocent could one have asked for?

A state producing precious metals, stones,
And—writing; none of these except perhaps
The precious literature in quantity
Or quality to worry the producer
About disposing of it. Do you know,
Considering the market, there are more
Poems produced than any other thing?
No wonder poets sometimes have to seem
So much more business-like than business men.
Their wares are so much harder to get rid of.

She’s one of the two best states in the Union.
Vermont’s the other. And the two have been
Yoke-fellows in the sap-yoke from of old
In many Marches. And they lie like wedges,
Thick end to thin end and thin end to thick end,
And are a figure of the way the strong
Of mind and strong of arm should fit together,
One thick where one is thin and vice versa.

For all her mountains fall a little short,
Her people not quite short enough for Art,
She’s still New Hampshire, a most restful state.

It’s restful to arrive at a decision,
And restful just to think about New Hampshire.
At present I am living in Vermont.
```

New Hampshire is a long title poem of more than four hundred lines, followed by the volume’s Notes and Grace Notes. This excerpt includes its opening satire of selling, the famous “specimens” passage, and its closing turn toward rest; read the full poem in the linked 1923 volume.

## Summary

The speaker introduces New Hampshire by contrasting it with places and people that advertise their climate, resources, politics, or ideas. New Hampshire’s virtue is precisely that its goods are scarce, local, and not available in commercial quantities.

As the poem continues, the defense of the state becomes self-criticism. The speaker admits that writers need conflict, that he carries dissatisfaction everywhere, and that even the mountains are not high enough. He ends by calling New Hampshire restful while revealing that he lives in Vermont.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem’s central meaning lies in its suspicion of turning everything into a product. Frost treats New Hampshire as a “specimen” rather than a commodity, but the poem’s own wit and vivid descriptions also advertise the place. Its praise is therefore affectionate and knowingly contradictory.

The speaker wants a home that resists commerce, publicity, and improvement, yet he is a professional poet whose “wares” must reach readers. The final claim of rest is earned only after the poem exposes how quickly the mind converts landscape, people, and art into objects of judgment.

## Themes

### Commerce and authenticity

The opening encounters mock people who sell the advantages of their states or causes. Against this salesmanship, New Hampshire offers unsprayed apples, small quantities of gold, and poems that are difficult to dispose of—an ideal of value that does not depend on scale or profit.

### Regional identity

Frost builds New Hampshire from towns, mountains, local characters, neighboring Vermont, and the speech of rural New England. The regional portrait is not simple boosterism: the speaker belongs to the place while teasing its people and admitting that he is an outsider even within his chosen home.

### Art and dissatisfaction

The speaker worries that comfortable people do not provide enough misery for serious literature, then recognizes this as a literary appetite rather than a moral truth. His dissatisfaction keeps the poem lively but prevents the restful state from becoming an uncomplicated refuge.

### Rest versus restlessness

The ending names New Hampshire “a most restful state,” yet the speaker has spent the poem measuring its mountains, people, and culture against impossible standards. Rest arrives as a decision and a temporary posture, not as the disappearance of criticism.

## Form and meter

The title poem is written in loose blank verse: unrhymed lines that generally draw on iambic pentameter but regularly loosen into conversational rhythms, extra syllables, and speech-like variation.

Its long, discursive form makes room for anecdotes, dialogue, literary allusions, jokes, and sudden meditations. The poem sounds like talk, but the recurring five-beat movement gives the talk a durable poetic frame.

The volume’s structure is part of the poem’s form. Footnote markers in the title poem point toward the separate Notes and Grace Notes, making the book a self-referential sequence rather than a standalone lyric.

## Literary devices

### Irony

> This is called having an idea to sell.

The speaker condemns salesmanship while selling the reader on New Hampshire through comic stories and memorable local details. That contradiction makes the poem’s praise more intelligent and less sentimental.

### Personification

> She’s one of the two best states in the Union.

New Hampshire becomes a woman who possesses, refuses, and speaks through the speaker’s portrait. Personification turns regional identity into a relationship with a character whose virtues and faults can be argued over.

### Catalog and anecdote

> One each of everything as in a show-case

Lists of presidents, minerals, towns, occupations, and odd local stories create the feeling of a traveling inventory. The catalog form both celebrates the state’s variety and mocks the impulse to measure it.

### Paradox

> Their wares are so much harder to get rid of.

The poem calls literature a commodity while emphasizing how resistant poems are to ordinary exchange. This paradox exposes the poet’s uneasy position inside the market he criticizes.

## Historical context

New Hampshire was published by Henry Holt in October 1923 as New Hampshire, A Poem with Notes and Grace Notes. The volume won the 1924 Pulitzer Prize for Poetry and includes the title poem, narrative Notes, and shorter Grace Notes.

Frost had moved from Massachusetts to New Hampshire, and the poem draws on the region’s towns, farms, mountains, folklore, and speech. Its mock-scholarly cross-references also appeared soon after T. S. Eliot’s The Waste Land, but Frost directs readers inward to poems in his own volume.

The first printing is dated 1923, placing the work in the US public domain. The linked Wikisource edition reproduces the 1923 volume and identifies the work as public domain in the United States.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of New Hampshire?

The poem praises New Hampshire as a place that resists commercialization, but its speaker also admits that he turns the state into material for art. The result is an affectionate, ironic portrait of a place valued for its limits and local character.

### What are the main themes in New Hampshire?

The main themes are commerce and authenticity, regional identity, art and dissatisfaction, and the tension between rest and restlessness. Frost treats landscape and community as sources of belonging without pretending they are perfect.

### What form and meter does New Hampshire use?

The long title poem uses loose blank verse: mostly iambic pentameter without end rhyme, varied by conversational phrasing, dialogue, and extra or missing syllables. Its footnotes connect it to the volume’s Notes and Grace Notes.

### Why is New Hampshire an excerpt on this page?

The title poem is more than four hundred lines long. This page presents a famous passage from its opening and ending while linking to the full 1923 text.
