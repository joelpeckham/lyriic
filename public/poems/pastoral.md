# Pastoral analysis

Pastoral — William Carlos Williams (1917)

This analysis explains how William Carlos Williams turns an American street into a modern pastoral, contrasting sparrows, ordinary labor, and religious authority. Read the full poem, its meaning, themes, form, and devices.

## The poem

```
The little sparrows
hop ingenuously
about the pavement
quarreling
with sharp voices
over those things
that interest them.
But we who are wiser
shut ourselves in
on either hand
and no one knows
whether we think good
or evil.
Meanwhile,
the old man who goes about
gathering dog-lime
walks in the gutter
without looking up
and his tread
is more majestic than
that of the Episcopal minister
approaching the pulpit
of a Sunday.
These things
astonish me beyond words.
```

Text from [Al Que Quiere! (1917), Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/51997/51997-0.txt). Public domain in the US (First published in Others in 1915 and included in the 1917 collection Al Que Quiere!, the poem is in the US public domain.).

## Summary

The speaker watches sparrows quarrel on a pavement, then contrasts their direct, instinctive lives with the hidden moral uncertainty of human beings. The poem shifts from birds to an old man gathering dog-lime in the gutter.

That laborer’s tread is called more majestic than the Episcopal minister’s approach to the pulpit. By placing an unglamorous street scene above a formal religious one, the poem makes ordinary life its own kind of pastoral subject.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem’s central surprise is a reversal of poetic and social hierarchy. A pastoral traditionally idealizes rural life, but Williams locates his version on an urban pavement and finds dignity in work that polite society might overlook.

The sparrows seem less troubled by moral abstraction than the people who shut themselves in. Their quarrel is noisy and limited, while human beings are “wiser” yet unknowable to one another. The comparison complicates the assumption that human reason automatically produces a better life.

The ending does not resolve the speaker’s astonishment. It records an encounter with value in the everyday rather than translating that value into a final doctrine; the phrase “beyond words” leaves perception active after the poem stops.

## Themes

### The ordinary as worthy

Sparrows, pavement, a gutter, and the collection of dog-lime receive sustained attention. Williams treats common urban details as material for serious poetry rather than as scenery beneath notice.

### Human uncertainty

The speaker calls people wiser than sparrows but admits that no one knows whether they think good or evil. The claim exposes the limits of social confidence and moral self-knowledge.

### Work and dignity

The old man’s physical task is uncelebrated, yet his steady tread appears more majestic than the minister’s ceremonial movement. The contrast gives dignity to useful labor without romanticizing its conditions.

### Religion and social hierarchy

The Episcopal minister represents institutional status and public ceremony. His comparison with the gutter worker questions whether official position is the same thing as moral or human importance.

## Form and meter

“Pastoral” is a single-stanza free-verse poem of twenty-five short lines. It has no regular rhyme scheme or fixed metrical pattern, so the reader’s pace comes from syntax, line breaks, and changing stresses.

The narrow lines make ordinary speech feel observed in fragments: “quarreling,” “or evil,” and “These things” each receive isolated emphasis. Enjambment carries the sentences forward while the short lines repeatedly interrupt that movement.

The poem’s two-part movement is marked by “Meanwhile,” which turns from the sparrows and enclosed humans to the old man and minister. The form therefore enacts a sequence of attention rather than a conventional stanzaic argument.

## Literary devices

### Juxtaposition

> his tread / is more majestic than / that of the Episcopal minister

The poem places a gutter worker beside a church official and reverses the expected ranking. The comparison makes the reader reconsider which actions deserve reverence.

### Contrast

> the little sparrows ... But we who are wiser

The speaker opposes the birds’ visible quarrel to the hidden thoughts of humans. The contrast questions whether apparent rationality is a meaningful advantage.

### Precise visual and auditory imagery

> about the pavement / quarreling / with sharp voices

Pavement and sharp voices establish a specific sensory scene. Williams’s clear details keep the poem grounded in an urban place instead of an idealized countryside.

### Irony

> These things / astonish me beyond words.

The ending uses language to declare that language is insufficient. The speaker’s astonishment is both the result of close observation and a refusal to flatten the scene into a simple lesson.

## Historical context

The poem appeared in Others in August 1915 and was included in Williams’s 1917 collection Al Que Quiere! The first-publication date and the 1917 book context explain why the catalog uses 1917 as its page year.

The Academy of American Poets describes Williams as a leading figure associated with Imagism. His short lines, precise details, and attention to American subjects connect this poem to the movement’s break from ornamental and conventional poetic language.

Williams was also a physician who lived and worked in Rutherford, New Jersey. The Poetry Foundation’s biographical account emphasizes his long medical practice and his commitment to American subjects, context that helps explain the poem’s interest in local streets and ordinary people.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of William Carlos Williams’s “Pastoral”?

The poem finds dignity and wonder in ordinary urban life. By comparing a man gathering dog-lime with an Episcopal minister, it questions social and religious hierarchies and makes everyday labor a serious poetic subject.

### What are the main themes in “Pastoral”?

Its main themes include the value of ordinary things, human moral uncertainty, the dignity of labor, and skepticism toward institutional status. The poem also reworks the pastoral tradition for an American city street.

### What form and meter does “Pastoral” use?

It is a single-stanza free-verse poem with twenty-five short lines. It has no regular rhyme scheme or fixed meter; line breaks, enjambment, syntax, and changing stresses shape its rhythm.

### Why is the old man more majestic than the minister?

The comparison is deliberately unexpected. The old man’s steady, useful labor gives him a physical and moral dignity that the minister’s formal religious role does not automatically possess.
