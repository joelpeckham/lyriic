# Song of Myself analysis

Song of Myself — Walt Whitman (1855)

Whitman’s Song of Myself turns a personal voice into an expansive claim about shared human identity. This analysis focuses on its meaning, themes, and free verse.

Full text from [Leaves of Grass (1855), “I celebrate myself”](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Leaves_of_Grass_(1855)/I_celebrate_myself). Public domain in the US (First published as “I celebrate myself” in Leaves of Grass (1855), before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The speaker celebrates himself while immediately linking his identity to the reader. The poem’s “I” expands through bodies, work, nature, death, and shared experience.

```
I celebrate myself,
And what I assume you shall assume,
```

When a child asks what grass means, the speaker offers several guesses rather than a final definition. The poem keeps knowledge open and experiential.[2]

## Meaning and interpretation

Whitman’s self-celebration is not meant to isolate the speaker. “Every atom” becomes a material image of equality between poet and reader.

```
For every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you.
```

Grass gathers possible meanings—common life, spiritual mystery, and continuity between the living and the dead—without settling into one symbol.[3]

## Themes

### Self and shared identity

The singular voice becomes a route into fellowship: the speaker asks readers to recognize their own material and spiritual participation in the world.

### Democracy and equality

Catalogues place bodies, occupations, places, and experiences in an expansive series. Formally and thematically, the poem resists a narrow hierarchy of subjects.[2]

### Nature and mystery

```
I lean and loafe at my ease .... observing a spear of summer grass.
```

Ordinary natural objects become occasions for thought, not decorations placed outside human experience.

## Form and meter

The poem uses free verse rather than a fixed meter or regular rhyme scheme. Breath-length lines, parallel syntax, anaphora, and catalogues supply its rhythm.

James E. Miller argues that the poem’s apparent looseness should not be mistaken for the absence of structure; he searches for an informing idea connecting its parts.[2]

## Literary devices

### Anaphora

```
I celebrate myself,
And what I assume you shall assume,
```

Repeated conjunctions and openings create additive forward motion, making each perception part of an unfinished whole.

### Catalogue

Whitman’s long lists widen the field of attention and give equality a formal shape by placing unlike people and sensations in one sequence.

### Symbolism

```
I guess it must be the flag of my disposition, out of hopeful green stuff woven.
```

Grass remains multivalent: it can suggest character, growth, divine communication, and shared material life.

## Historical context

Whitman self-published Leaves of Grass on July 4, 1855. The poem was initially untitled, and later editions changed its title, wording, and section divisions.[3]

The first edition’s democratic address and long lines helped establish Whitman’s distinct American poetic voice. The Poetry Foundation describes his method as adapting epic scale through connected lyric poems.[3]

## What critics say

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning?

Whitman presents individuality as connected to other people, the natural world, and shared material life.

### What does grass symbolize?

Grass has several possible meanings, including common life, spiritual mystery, growth, and continuity between living and dead bodies.

### What form does the poem use?

It uses free verse, with rhythm shaped by breath, syntax, repetition, and catalogues rather than fixed meter.
