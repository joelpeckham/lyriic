# Song of the Witches analysis

Song of the Witches — William Shakespeare (1606)

This Song of the Witches analysis explains how Shakespeare’s “Double, double, toil and trouble” chant uses meter, rhyme, and disturbing ingredients to create a ritual of disorder.

## The poem

```
Round about the cauldron go;
In the poison’d entrails throw.
Toad, that under cold stone
Days and nights hast thirty one
Swelter’d venom sleeping got,
Boil thou first i’ the charmed pot.

Double, double toil and trouble;
Fire burn and cauldron bubble.

Fillet of a fenny snake,
In the cauldron boil and bake;
Eye of newt and toe of frog,
Wool of bat and tongue of dog,
Adder’s fork and blind-worm’s sting,
Lizard’s leg and howlet’s wing,
For a charm of powerful trouble,
Like a hell-broth boil and bubble.

Double, double toil and trouble;
Fire burn and cauldron bubble.

Scale of dragon, tooth of wolf,
Witches’ mummy, maw and gulf
Of the ravin’d salt-sea shark,
Root of hemlock digg’d i’ the dark,
Liver of blaspheming Jew,
Gall of goat, and slips of yew
Silver’d in the moon’s eclipse,
Nose of Turk and Tartar’s lips,
Finger of birth-strangled babe
Ditch-deliver’d by a drab,
Make the gruel thick and slab:
Add thereto a tiger’s chaudron,
For the ingredients of our cauldron.

Double, double toil and trouble;
Fire burn and cauldron bubble.

Cool it with a baboon’s blood,
Then the charm is firm and good.
```

Text from [Macbeth, Act IV, Scene I](https://www.shakespeare-online.com/plays/macbeth_4_1.html). Public domain in the US (Macbeth was first performed and published in the early seventeenth century; William Shakespeare died in 1616, so this passage is public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

Three witches prepare a potion by naming poisonous, animal, human, and occult ingredients. Their repeated refrain turns the inventory into a spell, while the final baboon’s blood seals the charm.

The chant appears just before Macbeth asks the witches for prophecy. Its language makes evil feel manufactured: separate horrors are measured, mixed, thickened, and made ready for use.

## Meaning and interpretation

The chant’s “double” suggests both intensification and doubleness. The witches make trouble sound pleasurable and musical, while their later prophecies similarly conceal danger inside reassuring words.

The cauldron gathers a world in moral and physical disarray. Shakespeare’s catalogue moves from animal parts to human remains and blasphemous substances, making the potion a concentrated image of the violence Macbeth is about to embrace.

The refrain does not explain the witches’ power; it performs it. Repetition, rhyme, and a pounding falling rhythm turn language into ritual and make disorder sound organized.

## Themes

### Chaos made into ritual

The witches impose sequence on grotesque materials: throw, boil, add, thicken, and cool. Their methodical language gives supernatural disorder the shape of a recipe.

### Deception and doubleness

“Double” names the chant’s repeated intensification, but it also echoes the witches’ habit of speaking in misleading half-truths. The sing-song surface hides a destructive purpose.

### The corruption of nature

Snake, frog, bat, dragon, wolf, shark, goat, and yew are stripped of their ordinary identities and reduced to ingredients. The chant imagines nature as material that can be violated and recombined.

## Form and meter

The witches’ speeches are mainly written in trochaic tetrameter: four falling units that begin with a stressed syllable. Many lines are catalectic, ending before the final unstressed syllable, which gives the chant a clipped, incantatory force.

The passage uses rhyming couplets and recurring two-line refrains. The repeated “trouble / bubble” rhyme makes the spell circular, while the short imperatives and repeated sounds distinguish the witches from the play’s usual blank verse.

The meter is a performance tool as much as a pattern. Its strong-weak beat can sound like a chant, a march, or repeated stirring around the cauldron.

## Literary devices

### Refrain and repetition

> Double, double toil and trouble; / Fire burn and cauldron bubble.

The refrain returns after each major ingredient list. Its repetition makes the spell feel collective and iterative, as though each cycle increases the potion’s force.

### Catalogue

> Eye of newt and toe of frog, / Wool of bat and tongue of dog,

The accumulating list overwhelms the scene with sensory detail. The paired body parts create a grotesque rhythm while reducing living creatures to interchangeable components.

### Alliteration and plosive sound

> Double, double toil and trouble;

The repeated d and t sounds give the refrain hard verbal knocks. Alongside the b sounds in “burn,” “bubble,” and “broth,” they make the language percussive and memorable.

### Grotesque imagery

> Finger of birth-strangled babe / Ditch-deliver’d by a drab,

The chant escalates from animal ingredients to human remains. This shocking image makes the witches’ magic ethically repellent rather than merely exotic.

## Historical context

Macbeth was written and performed around 1606, during the reign of James I. Its witches and interest in prophecy fit an early modern culture deeply concerned with witchcraft, treason, kingship, and supernatural signs.

The passage occurs in Act IV, Scene I, after Macbeth has become a violent ruler. He now seeks the witches instead of merely encountering them, showing that his dependence on forbidden knowledge has grown.

The line “liver of blaspheming Jew” reflects antisemitic language in the early modern text. It should be preserved when presenting the historical public-domain passage, but identified as dehumanizing rather than repeated uncritically.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of “Double, double, toil and trouble”?

The refrain is an incantation that intensifies the witches’ work. “Double” also hints at deception and hidden meanings, which are central to the witches’ misleading prophecies.

### What are the main themes in the Song of the Witches?

The main themes are chaos organized into ritual, deceptive doubleness, supernatural evil, and the corruption of nature and human life.

### What meter does the witches’ chant use?

The chant is mainly trochaic tetrameter, often with catalectic lines. Its falling, clipped rhythm helps it sound unlike ordinary speech and unlike the play’s usual blank verse.

### Why do Shakespeare’s witches list strange ingredients?

The catalogue creates grotesque sensory imagery and turns violence into a recipe. The ingredients also make the witches’ supernatural power feel physical, deliberate, and morally corrupt.
