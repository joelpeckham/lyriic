# Sonnet 73 analysis

Sonnet 73 — William Shakespeare (1609)

This Sonnet 73 analysis explains how three diminishing images turn aging into an argument for intensified love.

Full text from [Sonnet 73](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45099/sonnet-73-that-time-of-year-thou-mayst-in-me-behold). Public domain in the US (Shakespeare’s Sonnets were first published in 1609 and are public domain.).

## Summary

The speaker asks the beloved to see him as late autumn, fading twilight, and a fire nearly consumed by its own fuel.[1]

```
That time of year thou mayst in me behold
When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang
```

The final couplet turns decline into an emotional claim: awareness of approaching separation makes love stronger.[2]

## Meaning and interpretation

The metaphors narrow in scale from a season to a day and then to the last glow of a fire. Mortality becomes increasingly immediate.

```
In me thou see’st the glowing of such fire
That on the ashes of his youth doth lie,
```

The couplet makes impermanence part of love’s value. What must soon be left is cherished more carefully now.[2]

## Themes

### Aging and mortality

Sparse leaves, vanishing light, and dying embers make bodily decline visible. “Death’s second self” joins ordinary sleep to mortality.

### Time and compression

```
Which by and by black night doth take away,
Death’s second self, that seals up all in rest.
```

Each quatrain measures a smaller remainder of time, compressing the speaker’s future.

### Love strengthened by loss

The closing argument is paradoxical: the beloved’s knowledge of impending separation intensifies attachment.

## Form and meter

This is a Shakespearean sonnet: three quatrains and a couplet, with ABAB CDCD EFEF GG rhyme and chiefly iambic pentameter.

The repeated “In me thou see’st” creates parallel structure, while the couplet turns image into conclusion.

## Literary devices

### Extended metaphor

```
In me thou see’st the twilight of such day
As after sunset fadeth in the west,
```

Autumn, twilight, and fire are successive versions of the same approach toward disappearance.

### Personification

```
Which by and by black night doth take away
```

Night acts as an agent that removes the remaining light, giving death an unavoidable force.

### Paradox

```
This thou perceiv’st, which makes thy love more strong,
To love that well which thou must leave ere long.
```

Weakening and approaching loss produce stronger love, making mortality emotionally productive.

## Historical context

The sonnets appeared in the 1609 Quarto. Sonnet 73 is traditionally associated with the Fair Youth sequence, though the poems do not establish a complete biography.

The poem’s “ruin’d choirs” can evoke both bare branches and stripped church interiors, layering personal aging with cultural memory.[2]

## What critics say

> “The fundamental emotion [in Sonnet 73] is self-pity. Not an attractive emotion. What renders it pathetic, in the good instead of the bad sense, is the sinister diminution of the time concept, quatrain by quatrain.”
>
> — John Berryman, [Shakespeare Online](https://www.shakespeare-online.com/sonnets/73detail.html)

> “The structure is good, the three quatrains offering distinct yet equivalent figures for the time of life of the unsuccessful and to-be-pitied lover.”
>
> — John Crowe Ransom, [Shakespeare Online](https://www.shakespeare-online.com/sonnets/73detail.html)

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning?

Three images of decline lead to the idea that approaching loss can make love more intense.

### What are the three metaphors?

The speaker compares himself to late autumn, fading twilight, and a dying fire.

### What is the form?

It is a Shakespearean sonnet in iambic pentameter with ABAB CDCD EFEF GG rhyme.
