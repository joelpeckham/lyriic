# The Cremation of Sam McGee analysis

The Cremation of Sam McGee — Robert W. Service (1907)

Robert W. Service's The Cremation of Sam McGee is a fast-moving Yukon narrative about a promise, survival, and an unforgettable reversal. This analysis explains its meaning, themes, ballad form, and dark humor.

## The poem

```
There are strange things done in the midnight sun
By the men who moil for gold;
The Arctic trails have their secret tales
That would make your blood run cold;
The Northern Lights have seen queer sights,
But the queerest they ever did see
Was that night on the marge of Lake Lebarge
I cremated Sam McGee.

Now Sam McGee was from Tennessee, where the cotton blooms and blows.
Why he left his home in the South to roam 'round the Pole, God only knows.
He was always cold, but the land of gold seemed to hold him like a spell;
Though he'd often say in his homely way that "he'd sooner live in hell."

On a Christmas Day we were mushing our way over the Dawson trail.
Talk of your cold! through the parka's fold it stabbed like a driven nail.
If our eyes we'd close, then the lashes froze till sometimes we couldn't see;
It wasn't much fun, but the only one to whimper was Sam McGee.

And that very night, as we lay packed tight in our robes beneath the snow,
And the dogs were fed, and the stars o'erhead were dancing heel and toe,
He turned to me, and "Cap," says he, "I'll cash in this trip, I guess;
And if I do, I'm asking that you won't refuse my last request."

Well, he seemed so low that I couldn't say no; then he says with a sort of moan:
"It's the cursed cold, and it's got right hold till I'm chilled clean through to the bone.
Yet 'tain't being dead—it's my awful dread of the icy grave that pains;
So I want you to swear that, foul or fair, you'll cremate my last remains."

A pal's last need is a thing to heed, so I swore I would not fail;
And we started on at the streak of dawn; but God! he looked ghastly pale.
He crouched on the sleigh, and he raved all day of his home in Tennessee;
And before nightfall a corpse was all that was left of Sam McGee.

There wasn't a breath in that land of death, and I hurried, horror-driven,
With a corpse half hid that I couldn't get rid, because of a promise given;
It was lashed to the sleigh, and it seemed to say: "You may tax your brawn and brains,
But you promised true, and it's up to you to cremate those last remains."

Now a promise made is a debt unpaid, and the trail has its own stern code.
In the days to come, though my lips were dumb, in my heart how I cursed that load.
In the long, long night, by the lone firelight, while the huskies, round in a ring,
Howled out their woes to the homeless snows—O God! how I loathed the thing.

And every day that quiet clay seemed to heavy and heavier grow;
And on I went, though the dogs were spent and the grub was getting low;
The trail was bad, and I felt half mad, but I swore I would not give in;
And I'd often sing to the hateful thing, and it hearkened with a grin.

Till I came to the marge of Lake Lebarge, and a derelict there lay;
It was jammed in the ice, but I saw in a trice it was called the "Alice May."
And I looked at it, and I thought a bit, and I looked at my frozen chum;
Then "Here," said I, with a sudden cry, "is my cre-ma-tor-eum."

Some planks I tore from the cabin floor, and I lit the boiler fire;
Some coal I found that was lying around, and I heaped the fuel higher;
The flames just soared, and the furnace roared—such a blaze you seldom see;
And I burrowed a hole in the glowing coal, and I stuffed in Sam McGee.

Then I made a hike, for I didn't like to hear him sizzle so;
And the heavens scowled, and the huskies howled, and the wind began to blow.
It was icy cold, but the hot sweat rolled down my cheeks, and I don't know why;
And the greasy smoke in an inky cloak went streaking down the sky.

I do not know how long in the snow I wrestled with grisly fear;
But the stars came out and they danced about ere again I ventured near;
I was sick with dread, but I bravely said: "I'll just take a peep inside.
I guess he's cooked, and it's time I looked"; . . . then the door I opened wide.

And there sat Sam, looking cool and calm, in the heart of the furnace roar;
And he wore a smile you could see a mile, and he said: "Please close that door.
It's fine in here, but I greatly fear you'll let in the cold and storm—
Since I left Plumtree, down in Tennessee, it's the first time I've been warm."

There are strange things done in the midnight sun
By the men who moil for gold;
The Arctic trails have their secret tales
That would make your blood run cold;
The Northern Lights have seen queer sights,
But the queerest they ever did see
Was that night on the marge of Lake Lebarge
I cremated Sam McGee.
```

## Summary

The narrator, known as Cap, and Sam McGee are prospectors mushing along the Dawson trail in brutal Yukon cold. Sam, terrified of an icy grave, makes Cap promise to cremate him if he dies; by nightfall, Sam is a corpse.

Cap hauls the body to Lake Lebarge, finds the derelict Alice May, and uses its boiler as a makeshift crematorium. The apparent horror turns into comic relief when Sam is found alive inside, enjoying the warmth.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem treats loyalty as a practical law of the trail. Cap dislikes the burden and even calls the body a hateful thing, but “a promise made is a debt unpaid” gives his suffering a moral shape: friendship means carrying out a request even when it is frightening or absurd.

The twist does not erase the poem's earlier danger. Its humor grows out of the same cold that nearly kills Sam, so the ending makes warmth feel like a reversal of the Yukon world rather than a conventional rescue. The poem's meaning rests in that tension between grim survival and comic storytelling.

## Themes

### Friendship and loyalty

Cap's actions are driven by Sam's last request. The repeated language of pals, promises, and the trail's code frames loyalty as a duty that must be honored through exhaustion and disgust.

### Survival in an unforgiving landscape

The poem's cold is physical, psychological, and social: it freezes eyelashes, drains the dogs, isolates the travelers, and turns a dead companion into a load Cap must drag across the wilderness.

### Death and dark comedy

Service builds a Gothic situation with a corpse, a furnace, and grisly fear, then breaks the tension with Sam's complaint about letting in the cold. The comic ending depends on the seriousness of the threat that came before it.

### The lure of gold

Sam leaves a warm Tennessee landscape for the Yukon because the “land of gold” holds him like a spell. The poem balances the promise of wealth against the bodily cost of pursuing it.

## Form and meter

The poem is a narrative ballad with a repeated eight-line opening and closing stanza. Its central story is arranged in shorter quatrain-like units, while the framing refrain gives the tale a legendary, fireside-story shape.

The dominant movement is anapestic tetrameter, or four rising rhythmic beats, though Service varies the line lengths and stresses to keep the diction conversational. The rolling rhythm suits mushing, travel, and oral recitation.

Frequent internal rhyme and paired end rhymes create momentum: the sound carries the reader forward even when the narrative becomes grim. The repeated opening stanza makes the ending feel like a return to the poem's strange Yukon folklore.

## Literary devices

### Simile

> through the parka's fold it stabbed like a driven nail.

The comparison gives cold the force of a physical weapon. It turns weather into an active antagonist and prepares the reader for Sam's fear of the icy grave.

### Internal rhyme

> The flames just soared, and the furnace roared—such a blaze you seldom see;

Rhyme within the line makes the narration quick and memorable. It also lets violent or frightening events sound like a performed ballad rather than a detached report.

### Personification

> the trail has its own stern code.

The trail becomes an authority that judges the travelers. This personification makes Cap's promise feel larger than a private agreement: it is part of the culture of survival in the North.

### Irony and reversal

> Since I left Plumtree, down in Tennessee, it's the first time I've been warm.

Sam's calm response reverses the expected result of cremation. The line is funny because it states the grim premise literally while revealing that warmth, not death, is what Sam has wanted all along.

## Historical context

The poem first appeared in Service's 1907 collection Songs of a Sourdough, written out of the Yukon world associated with the Klondike Gold Rush. Its public-domain status in the United States allows the complete 1907 text to be shown here.

The story is fictional but draws on a real incident involving Service's roommate, Dr. Leonard S. E. Sugden, who cremated a miner's body in the firebox of a derelict steamer. Service changed the details into the Alice May, Lake Lebarge, and the comic discovery of Sam alive.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of The Cremation of Sam McGee?

The poem presents friendship as a promise that must be honored even in extreme conditions. Its comic ending also shows how dark fear can be transformed into a memorable story.

### What are the main themes in The Cremation of Sam McGee?

The main themes are loyalty, survival in the Yukon, the lure of gold, death, and dark comedy. The poem places the warmth of friendship and humor against an unforgiving landscape.

### What form and meter does the poem use?

It is a narrative ballad with a repeated opening and closing stanza, strong rhyme, and a mainly anapestic tetrameter rhythm. Service varies the meter for natural speech and dramatic emphasis.

### Is Sam McGee really dead at the end of the poem?

No. Cap expects to find Sam's body burned, but Sam is alive inside the furnace and says it is the first time he has been warm since leaving Tennessee.
