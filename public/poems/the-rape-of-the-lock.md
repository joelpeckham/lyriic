# The Rape of the Lock analysis

The Rape of the Lock — Alexander Pope (1714)

Pope turns a stolen lock of hair into a mock epic whose heroic couplets expose fashionable vanity and conflict.

Full text from [The Rape of the Lock and other poems](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/9800/9800-h/9800-h.htm). Public domain in the US (First published in 1712 and expanded in 1714, centuries before the US cutoff.).

## Summary

Belinda attends fashionable society, plays ombre, and becomes the target of the Baron, who cuts a lock of her hair. Sylphs guard her, but epic machinery cannot prevent the social crisis.

```
Swift to the Lock a thousand Sprites repair,
A thousand wings, by turns, blow back the hair;
And thrice they twitch'd the diamond in her ear;
```

The poem’s grand style makes a small social incident look enormous, revealing how status and reputation govern its world.[2]

## Meaning and interpretation

Its central joke is a mismatch between subject and style: cards, cosmetics, and scissors receive the language of war and epic destiny.[2]

```
Let Spades be trumps! she said, and trumps they were.
Now move to war her sable Matadores,
```

The satire is amused but not empty. The Baron’s language of conquest makes desire and possession visible beneath the comedy.[3]

## Themes

### Triviality and social importance

A lock is materially small but becomes a crisis of honor.

### Gender and possession

Epic battle language turns a fashionable flirtation into a contest over a woman’s body and reputation.

### Vanity and performance

Cards, fans, cosmetics, and conversation make identity a public performance.[3]

## Form and meter

The five-canto mock epic is chiefly written in heroic couplets: rhymed pairs of iambic pentameter.

Invocation, supernatural guardians, battle scenes, speeches, and elevated diction imitate epic conventions while miniaturizing them around fashionable life.[2]

## Literary devices

### Mock epic

```
And particolour'd troops, a shining train,
Draw forth to combat on the velvet plain.
```

The card table becomes a battlefield, creating comic disproportion.

### Bathos

```
Here Britain's statesmen oft the fall foredoom
Of foreign Tyrants and of Nymphs at home;
```

The movement from national politics to gossip deflates grandeur.

### Personification

```
T' inclose the Lock; now joins it, to divide.
```

The scissors become a heroic weapon in a miniature encounter.

## Historical context

Pope wrote the poem after a quarrel between Arabella Fermor and Lord Robert Petre. He published two cantos in 1712 and expanded the work to five in 1714.[3]

The early eighteenth-century setting includes Hampton Court, ombre, cosmetics, coffee, and elaborate dress, giving the satire a concrete social target.

## Common questions

### Why is it called a mock epic?

It imitates epic conventions while applying them to cards, cosmetics, gossip, and scissors.

### What is the main meaning?

The poem satirizes vanity, status anxiety, and possessive social conflict through comic disproportion.

### What form does it use?

It is a five-canto poem chiefly written in heroic couplets.
