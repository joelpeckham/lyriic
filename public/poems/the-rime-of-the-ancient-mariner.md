# The Rime of the Ancient Mariner analysis

The Rime of the Ancient Mariner — Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1798)

Samuel Taylor Coleridge's The Rime of the Ancient Mariner turns an inexplicable act at sea into a haunting study of guilt, nature, and attempted redemption. This analysis explains its meaning, themes, form, and symbolism.

## The poem

```
It is an ancyent Marinere,
And he stoppeth one of three:
“By thy long grey beard and glittering eye
Now wherefore stopp’st thou me?

The Bridegroom’s doors are open’d wide,
And I am next of kin;
The Guests are met, the Feast is set,—
May’st hear the merry din.”

He holds him with his glittering eye—
The Wedding-Guest stood still,
And listens like a three years’ child:
The Marinere hath his will.

The Wedding-Guest sate on a stone:
He cannot chuse but hear;
And thus spake on that ancient man,
The bright-eyed Mariner.

The ship was cheered, the harbour cleared,
Merrily did we drop
Below the kirk, below the hill,
Below the lighthouse top.

The Ice was here, the Ice was there,
The Ice was all around:
It crack’d and growl’d, and roar’d and howl’d—
Like noises in a swound!

At length did cross an Albatross,
Thorough the Fog it came;
And an it were a Christian Soul,
We hail’d it in God’s name.

The Marineres gave it biscuit-worms,
And round and round it flew:
The Ice did split with a Thunder-fit;
The Helmsman steer’d us thro’.

And a good south wind sprung up behind;
The Albatross did follow;
And every day, for food or play,
Came to the Marinere’s hollo!

In mist or cloud, on mast or shroud,
It perch’d for vespers nine;
Whiles all the night, through fog-smoke white,
Glimmer’d the white Moon-shine.

“God save thee, ancyent Marinere!
From the fiends that plague thee thus!
Why look’st thou so?”—With my cross-bow
I shot the Albatross.
```

This excerpt presents the frame narrative, the voyage into the ice, the albatross's arrival and welcome, and the Mariner's unexplained shooting of the bird. The complete seven-part poem is available at the linked 1798 text source.

## Summary

An ancient mariner stops a wedding guest and compels him to hear a sea story. After the ship reaches ice and fog, an albatross appears, and the crew welcomes it as a Christian soul.

The mariner suddenly kills the harmless bird with his crossbow. That unexplained act begins the poem's supernatural ordeal, in which the crew suffers, the mariner bears the albatross as a sign of guilt, and his later blessing of living creatures begins an incomplete process of penance.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem's meaning is not exhausted by the simple lesson that the mariner should not have killed the albatross. The act is arbitrary: the narrative gives no motive, which makes the resulting guilt feel like an encounter with mortality, responsibility, and consequences that cannot be neatly explained.

Coleridge's poem holds moral clarity and imaginative ambiguity together. The mariner eventually says that prayer is bound to loving “man and bird and beast,” but his endless compulsion to retell the story shows that recognition does not erase harm or restore him to his former life.

## Themes

### Guilt and penance

The dead albatross becomes a visible sign of an act whose consequences fall on the whole crew. The mariner's punishment is not a single sentence completed once, but a recurring obligation to tell the tale.

### Nature and spiritual interdependence

The albatross is both a living animal and a figure the sailors interpret through religious language. The poem links violence toward the natural world with spiritual disorder, then makes renewed attention to living creatures part of the mariner's recovery.

### The supernatural and the unknowable

Storms, spirits, Death, and Life-in-Death create a world that exceeds ordinary explanation. Rather than making every event clear, the supernatural intensifies the poem's questions about why suffering follows an apparently senseless choice.

### Storytelling and compulsion

The mariner's story interrupts a wedding and changes the wedding guest. His repeated retellings make narrative both a burden and the only form of witness available to him.

## Form and meter

The poem is a supernatural narrative ballad arranged in seven parts. Its short stanzas, repeated phrases, dialogue, archaic diction, and abrupt changes of scene create the feeling of an oral tale being performed.

Much of the poem draws on alternating four-stress and three-stress ballad lines, but Coleridge varies line length, syntax, rhyme, and rhythm extensively. The irregularity suits a story that moves between ordinary speech, incantation, nightmare, and theological reflection.

The 1798 first edition differs from later versions, especially the 1817 text with its marginal glosses. This excerpt follows the 1798 Lyrical Ballads spelling and punctuation rather than silently combining editions.

## Literary devices

### Frame narrative

> He holds him with his glittering eye—

The mariner's interruption of the wedding guest creates a story within a story. The guest's forced attention also gives the reader a character whose response registers the tale's psychological effect.

### Symbolism

> I shot the Albatross.

The albatross becomes a symbol of violated life, arbitrary guilt, and the burden of consequence. Its meaning expands as the mariner's punishment continues beyond the original act.

### Repetition and refrain

> The Ice was here, the Ice was there,

Repetition turns description into incantation and emphasizes confinement. Recurrent formulas also echo oral ballad traditions while making the voyage feel cyclical and inescapable.

### Simile and sound imagery

> Like noises in a swound!

The ice is made audible through verbs such as “crack’d,” “growl’d,” “roar’d,” and “howl’d.” The comparison to a swoon blurs physical weather and altered consciousness.

## Historical context

The poem first appeared in the 1798 edition of Lyrical Ballads, the collaborative volume associated with Coleridge and William Wordsworth and often treated as a landmark of British Romantic poetry.

Coleridge revised the poem repeatedly. The 1817 version is especially recognizable for its marginal glosses, while the first edition uses the title and spellings “The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere.” This page identifies the 1798 publication while making the edition of the excerpt explicit.

The poem's sea voyage, polar ice, supernatural punishment, and archaic ballad voice combine older literary conventions with Romantic interest in intense feeling, imagination, and the relation between human beings and the natural world.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of The Rime of the Ancient Mariner?

The poem examines guilt, responsibility, and the difficult possibility of redemption after the mariner kills an albatross for no stated reason. It connects spiritual recovery with learning to value living things, while leaving the supernatural causes of the punishment partly mysterious.

### What does the albatross symbolize?

The albatross symbolizes living nature, violated innocence, and the burden of consequence. Once the mariner kills it, the bird becomes a public sign of guilt and a reminder that his action has affected more than himself.

### What are the main themes in the poem?

The central themes include guilt and penance, humanity's relationship with nature, the supernatural, and the compulsive power of storytelling. The poem also explores how moral meaning can coexist with uncertainty.

### What form and meter does The Rime of the Ancient Mariner use?

It is a seven-part supernatural narrative ballad. Much of its verse uses alternating four-stress and three-stress lines, but Coleridge varies the rhythm, rhyme, stanza shape, and line length, so it is better treated as an irregular ballad form than as a single strict meter.
