# The River-Merchant's Wife: A Letter analysis

The River-Merchant's Wife: A Letter — Ezra Pound, after Li Bai (1915)

This analysis explains how Ezra Pound's translation uses childhood memory, precise natural images, and free verse to portray love deepened by separation.

## The poem

```
While my hair was still cut straight across my forehead
I played about the front gate, pulling flowers.
You came by on bamboo stilts, playing horse,
You walked about my seat, playing with blue plums.
And we went on living in the village of Chokan:
Two small people, without dislike or suspicion.

At fourteen I married My Lord you.
I never laughed, being bashful.
Lowering my head, I looked at the wall.
Called to, a thousand times, I never looked back.

At fifteen I stopped scowling,
I desired my dust to be mingled with yours
Forever and forever, and forever.
Why should I climb the look out?

At sixteen you departed,
You went into far Ku-to-Yen, by the river of swirling eddies,
And you have been gone five months.
The monkeys make sorrowful noise overhead.

You dragged your feet when you went out.
By the gate now, the moss is grown, the different mosses,
Too deep to clear them away!
The leaves fall early this autumn, in wind.
The paired butterflies are already yellow with August
Over the grass in the West garden,
They hurt me,
I grow older,
If you are coming down through the narrows of the river Kiang,
Please let me know beforehand,
And I will come out to meet you,
As far as Cho-fu-Sa.
```

Text from [Cathay](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50155/50155-h/50155-h.htm). Public domain in the US (Pound's English translation was first published in Cathay in 1915, before January 1, 1931, and the source text is identified as public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The speaker addresses her absent husband by remembering the relationship from childhood. Their playful closeness becomes marriage, and marriage becomes a painful period of waiting after he leaves on a commercial journey.

The poem's emotional movement is gradual rather than dramatic: affection grows through shared history, while the abandoned home gathers moss and autumn. The final request asks for advance notice of his return, but the reunion remains only imagined.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem presents separation as a way of discovering the depth of love. The speaker first recalls a childhood without suspicion, then describes the desire for her dust to be mingled with his. Her present loneliness makes those ordinary memories newly intense.

Pound's version is best read as a translation and a modernist re-creation, not as a transparent equivalent of Li Bai's Chinese poem. The concrete images and compressed transitions make an ancient emotional situation immediate in English, while the mediation through Ernest Fenollosa's notes raises questions about what translation preserves and what it changes.

## Themes

### Love and separation

The speaker's account moves from games at the gate to married devotion and then to absence. The husband's departure gives the earlier scenes their emotional weight: remembered play becomes evidence of a bond she cannot replace.

### Time and changing identity

The repeated ages—fourteen, fifteen, and sixteen—organize the speaker's growth. The final statement, “I grow older,” compresses the five months of waiting into a larger awareness that love, marriage, and selfhood are changing together.

### Nature as emotional reflection

Moss, falling leaves, autumn butterflies, and sorrowful monkeys turn the landscape into a register of waiting. Nature does not simply mirror the speaker's feeling; its seasonal change makes absence visible and measurable.

### Translation and cultural mediation

The names “Rihaku,” “Chokan,” and “Ku-to-Yen” signal the poem's route through Chinese material, Japanese forms, Fenollosa's notes, and Pound's English. Its power as an English poem and its distance from the source tradition should be considered together.

## Form and meter

The poem is written in free verse, with no fixed rhyme scheme or regular metrical pattern. Its uneven stanza lengths and conversational syntax make the poem feel like a letter spoken from memory rather than a formally closed lyric.

Pound gives each line a strong image or syntactic movement: bamboo stilts, blue plums, moss, butterflies, and the river. Repetition—especially “Forever and forever, and forever”—creates rhythm without imposing a traditional meter.

The five uneven stanzas move from childhood recollection to marriage, devotion, departure, and the long final scene of waiting. The final conditional request leaves the poem emotionally open: the speaker plans a meeting, but does not receive an answer.

## Literary devices

### Concrete imagery

> You came by on bamboo stilts, playing horse,

The bamboo stilts and blue plums make the remembered courtship tactile and visible. Such objects carry the relationship more effectively than abstract declarations would.

### Anaphora and repetition

> At fourteen... / At fifteen... / At sixteen...

The repeated age markers give the speaker's story a simple chronology. The repetition of “forever” then stretches that chronology toward a promise that the present separation tests.

### Seasonal symbolism

> The leaves fall early this autumn, in wind.

Autumn suggests decline and the passage of time, while the early leaf-fall makes the speaker's waiting seem premature and unnatural. The season becomes part of the pressure of absence.

### Personification

> The monkeys make sorrowful noise overhead.

The monkeys' sound is described through the speaker's sorrow. Their cries seem to join her lament, showing how grief can transform ordinary sounds into emotional evidence.

## Historical context

Ezra Pound published Cathay in 1915. Its title page described the poems as translations “for the most part from the Chinese of Rihaku,” Pound's form of Li Bai's name. The River-Merchant's Wife: A Letter is based on Li Bai's eighth-century poem Changgan Xing.

Pound worked from Ernest Fenollosa's notes and from decipherings by Professors Mori and Ariga rather than translating directly from Chinese. Yale's Modernism Lab notes that Pound was not proficient in Chinese and often altered or omitted details while shaping the material into a new English poem.

The poem appeared during the First World War. Hugh Kenner's reading of Cathay as a book of war poetry places its stories of departure, exile, and waiting in a wider culture of separation, although that interpretation remains debated.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of The River-Merchant's Wife: A Letter?

The poem shows how separation reveals and intensifies love. The speaker's memories of childhood and marriage become more painful as the absent husband and the changing seasons make time tangible.

### What are the main themes of the poem?

The main themes are love, separation, time, memory, and nature. The poem also invites questions about translation because Pound re-created Li Bai's poem through Fenollosa's notes.

### What form does The River-Merchant's Wife: A Letter use?

It uses free verse, with uneven stanzas, no fixed rhyme scheme, and no regular meter. Repeated ages, images, and phrases provide structure in place of a traditional pattern.

### Is Pound's poem a direct translation of Li Bai?

No. It is a loose translation or re-creation based on Fenollosa's notes and other decipherings. It is valuable as a modernist English poem, but it should not be treated as a word-for-word rendering of the Chinese original.
