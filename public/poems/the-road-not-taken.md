# The Road Not Taken analysis

The Road Not Taken — Robert Frost (1916)

Frost’s famous choice poem is less simple advice than an unsettled meditation on similar alternatives and retrospective storytelling.

Full text from [The Road Not Taken](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44272/the-road-not-taken). Public domain in the US (First published in 1915 and collected in 1916, before the US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

A traveler reaches two roads and chooses one, while admitting that the paths were worn about the same. The final stanza imagines a future retelling that gives the choice a “difference.”

```
Two roads diverged in a yellow wood,
And sorry I could not travel both
And be one traveler, long I stood
```

The poem’s tension lies between the uncertain scene and the confident story later told about it.[2]

## Meaning and interpretation

It is not simply an instruction to choose an unconventional path. The speaker’s own evidence undercuts the claim that one road was less traveled.[2]

```
Though as for that the passing there
Had worn them really about the same,
```

The “sigh” can hold regret, satisfaction, or self-dramatization. Frost leaves the emotional meaning unresolved.[3]

## Themes

### Choice and irreversibility

One way leads to another, making return and direct knowledge of the alternative unlikely.

### Hindsight

A later narrative turns an uncertain impulse into a meaningful life story.[2]

### Regret and ambiguity

```
I shall be telling this with a sigh
Somewhere ages and ages hence:
```

The sigh refuses a single emotional interpretation.

## Form and meter

The poem has four five-line stanzas and an ABAAB rhyme scheme in each stanza.

Its base is iambic tetrameter, varied toward conversational speech; the long opening sentence mirrors deliberation.[2]

## Literary devices

### Extended metaphor

```
Two roads diverged in a yellow wood,
```

The fork becomes a metaphor for life alternatives while remaining an ordinary, uncertain scene.

### Irony

```
I took the one less traveled by,
```

The famous claim conflicts with the earlier admission that the roads were about the same.

### Repetition

```
And be one traveler
```

Repeated “and” and “I” make the speaker’s thought circle and divide.

## Historical context

Frost wrote the poem in 1915; it appeared in Mountain Interval in 1916. He wrote it as a joke for Edward Thomas, whose indecision on walks inspired the speaker.

The Poetry Foundation records Frost’s complaint that readers took the poem seriously despite his intended mock sigh.[2]

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning?

It explores uncertain choices and the stories people create about them afterward.

### Is it advice to take the less-traveled road?

Not simply: the poem says the roads were really about the same.

### Why does the speaker sigh?

The sigh is deliberately ambiguous, possibly regretful, satisfied, wistful, or theatrical.
