# The Second Coming analysis

The Second Coming — William Butler Yeats (1920)

This analysis explains how Yeats turns a falcon’s lost command, a widening gyre, and a sphinx-like rough beast into a vision of civilization entering a terrifying new cycle.

## The poem

```
Turning and turning in the widening gyre
The falcon cannot hear the falconer;
Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;
Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,
The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere
The ceremony of innocence is drowned;
The best lack all conviction, while the worst
Are full of passionate intensity.

Surely some revelation is at hand;
Surely the Second Coming is at hand.
The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out
When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi
Troubles my sight: somewhere in sands of the desert
A shape with lion body and the head of a man,
A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,
Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it
Reel shadows of the indignant desert birds.
The darkness drops again; but now I know
That twenty centuries of stony sleep
Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,
And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,
Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?
```

Text from [The Second Coming](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/E910001-066.html). Public domain in the US (First published in The Dial in November 1920; the poem is in the US public domain.).

## Summary

The first stanza describes a world losing its organizing centre. A falcon has flown beyond the falconer’s call, anarchy and violence spread, innocence is drowned, and the people best equipped to resist seem passive while destructive forces act with energy.

The speaker expects a Christian revelation, but the vision that arrives is a desert creature with a lion’s body and a man’s head. The poem ends by asking what rough beast is moving toward Bethlehem to begin the next historical age.

## Meaning and interpretation

The widening gyre is both a literal spiral and an image of Yeats’s cyclical theory of history. As the falcon moves beyond the falconer’s hearing, the relationship between motion and control breaks down; the image becomes a compact metaphor for a civilization no longer governed by its former principles.

“Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold” compresses political, moral, and spiritual collapse into one memorable statement. The following blood tide and drowned ceremony make the crisis collective: innocence is not simply lost by individuals but overwhelmed by forces moving through the world.

The second stanza revises the expected Christian Second Coming. Instead of a savior, the speaker sees a pitiless hybrid whose arrival is an anti-Nativity. Yeats leaves the beast’s identity unresolved, so it can signify the violent, dehumanized historical force born when an old order reaches its limit.

## Themes

### Historical cycles and the gyre

Yeats’s gyre is a spiral or vortex that represents a phase of history expanding toward crisis. The poem catches one cycle at the point where its centre can no longer contain the forces it has released.

### The collapse of authority

The falcon cannot hear the falconer, and the best lack conviction. Communication, leadership, and moral confidence all fail at once, leaving passionate intensity to the worst actors.

### Apocalypse and reversed revelation

The title invokes Christian prophecy, but the revelation is frightening rather than redemptive. Bethlehem becomes the destination of a new birth whose consequences are unknown and ominous.

## Form and meter

The poem has two stanzas: an eight-line opening and a fourteen-line second stanza. The second stanza’s sonnet-like length does not produce a conventional sonnet structure; the poem’s form feels deliberately unstable as the vision exceeds containment.

It is often described as blank verse, but the iambic pentameter is irregular. The opening lines use substitutions and extra syllables, while the vision’s long syntactic units strain the meter. This pressure makes formal instability echo the poem’s collapsing order.

There is no settled end-rhyme scheme. Near-rhymes and repeated sounds create partial links without restoring a neat pattern, while enjambment carries the reader through the tide, vision, and final question.

## Literary devices

### Symbolism

> Turning and turning in the widening gyre

The gyre symbolizes Yeats’s cyclical movement of history. Its widening motion suggests expansion away from a stable centre and toward a violent transition.

### Extended metaphor

> The falcon cannot hear the falconer

The falcon’s separation from its trainer turns a familiar image of control into a picture of failed communication and a civilization that has exceeded its guiding authority.

### Allusion and inversion

> Surely the Second Coming is at hand

The title and Bethlehem invoke Christian eschatology and the birth of Christ. The expected savior is replaced by an unnamed beast, making the poem’s revelation a dark inversion of the Nativity.

### Apostrophe and repetition

> Surely some revelation is at hand; / Surely the Second Coming is at hand.

The repeated “Surely” sounds like insistence mixed with desperation. Repetition briefly creates expectation before the speaker’s vision overturns it.

### Rhetorical question

> And what rough beast, its hour come round at last, / Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

The closing question refuses to name or explain the beast. Its slow verb and unanswered syntax leave the new age approaching rather than completed.

## Historical context

Yeats wrote the poem in January 1919, amid the aftermath of World War I, political unrest in Ireland, and the influenza pandemic. It was first published in The Dial in November 1920 and later appeared in Michael Robartes and the Dancer (1921).

The poem draws on Yeats’s mystical system of interlocking gyres, developed most fully in A Vision. In that framework, historical eras move through cycles; the poem imagines the Christian era’s two thousand years reaching a moment of violent transition.

The poem’s references to Spiritus Mundi, Bethlehem, and a new birth combine occult, biblical, and mythic language. The result is not a precise political forecast but an intentionally open image that later readers can apply to different crises.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of “The Second Coming”?

Yeats presents a civilization losing its moral and political centre as one historical cycle ends. The expected Christian renewal is replaced by the frightening arrival of an unknown rough beast.

### What does the gyre mean in “The Second Coming”?

The gyre is a spiral image for Yeats’s theory that history moves through recurring cycles. The widening gyre suggests an era moving away from its centre and toward upheaval.

### What does “Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold” mean?

The line means that the forces holding society together have failed. Order, authority, shared values, and communication can no longer contain the violence spreading through the world.

### Who or what is the rough beast?

The rough beast is a deliberately ambiguous, sphinx-like figure with a lion’s body and a man’s head. It represents the brutal new force or historical era that may emerge after the collapse of the old one.
