# The Song of Wandering Aengus analysis

The Song of Wandering Aengus — W. B. Yeats (1899)

W. B. Yeats's The Song of Wandering Aengus follows a magical encounter into a lifelong search for beauty, love, and an ideal that may never be reached. This analysis explains the poem's meaning, themes, imagery, and form.

## The poem

```
I went out to the hazel wood,
Because a fire was in my head,
And cut and peeled a hazel wand,
And hooked a berry to a thread;
And when white moths were on the wing,
And moth-like stars were flickering out,
I dropped the berry in a stream
And caught a little silver trout.

When I had laid it on the floor
I went to blow the fire a-flame,
But something rustled on the floor,
And someone called me by my name:
It had become a glimmering girl
With apple blossom in her hair
Who called me by my name and ran
And faded through the brightening air.

Though I am old with wandering
Through hollow lands and hilly lands,
I will find out where she has gone,
And kiss her lips and take her hands;
And walk among long dappled grass,
And pluck till time and times are done,
The silver apples of the moon,
The golden apples of the sun.
```

Text from [The Song of Wandering Aengus — Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Song_of_Wandering_Aengus). Public domain in the US (Collected in The Wind Among the Reeds in 1899; works first published in the United States by 1930 are public domain.).

## Summary

The speaker goes into a hazel wood, makes a simple fishing rod, and catches a silver trout. When he turns from the fire, the trout has become a glimmering girl who calls his name and disappears.

Now old from wandering, he remains certain that he will find her. His imagined reunion joins physical intimacy with a harvest of the moon's silver apples and the sun's golden apples.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem treats desire as both an interruption and a life direction. A mysterious impulse sends the speaker into the woods, and the fish's transformation gives that impulse a face. Because the girl vanishes almost as soon as she appears, the speaker spends his life pursuing an ideal shaped by one fleeting vision.

The ending does not settle whether the search is futile or spiritually sustaining. The silver and golden apples suggest a perfected, imaginative realm: the speaker may be seeking a person, a supernatural figure, or the beauty and creative vision that art can preserve. His certainty that he will find her keeps hope alive while also revealing the persistence of obsession.

## Themes

### Longing and unattainable desire

The speaker's encounter lasts only a moment, but it governs the rest of his life. His wandering shows how an ideal can become more powerful precisely because it remains out of reach.

### Myth and transformation

An ordinary trout becomes a named, shining girl, moving the poem from practical fishing into mythic vision. Yeats gives the ancient figure of Aengus a personal quest rather than presenting a complete traditional legend.

### Time, age, and eternity

The speaker is already old, yet his future verbs remain forceful: he will find, kiss, take, walk, and pluck. Mortal aging is set against the repeated promise of “time and times” and the enduring images of moon and sun.

## Form and meter

The poem has three eight-line stanzas. Each stanza uses alternating end-rhymes, with some looseness in the opening pairings, while repeated conjunctions and parallel phrasing create a ballad-like forward motion.

Its underlying movement is mostly iambic tetrameter, four rising beats per line, though Yeats varies the rhythm with extra syllables, substitutions, pauses, and longer compound phrases. The conversational meter lets the supernatural event arrive without a change in the speaker's plain narrative voice.

The poem's syntax also develops across the stanzas: practical actions lead to sudden transformation, then to a long vow projected into the future. The final stanza's repeated “And” makes the search feel continuous and unfinished.

## Literary devices

### Symbolism

> The silver apples of the moon,

The silver and golden apples suggest perfected beauty, celestial mystery, and a harvest that cannot be gathered in ordinary human time. Their pairing of moon and sun gives the speaker's desired world a total, almost eternal scale.

### Transformation

> It had become a glimmering girl

The trout's sudden change turns a small fishing scene into a supernatural encounter. The transformation also makes desire unstable: what the speaker catches is not what he finally wants.

### Sensory imagery

> With apple blossom in her hair

The poem combines touch, color, light, and natural fragrance in its images of the girl and the landscape. This sensory richness makes the brief vision feel vivid enough to justify a lifetime of memory.

### Repetition and parallelism

> Who called me by my name and ran

Repeated structures such as “called me by my name” and the sequence “kiss,” “take,” “walk,” and “pluck” connect recognition, pursuit, and imagined reunion. The syntax keeps the speaker moving toward an event that never occurs within the poem.

## Historical context

The poem was first printed in 1897 under the title “A Mad Song” and was collected under its familiar title in The Wind Among the Reeds in 1899. The Poetry Foundation identifies its source collection and marks the poem public domain.

Yeats wrote within a sustained interest in Irish folklore, mythology, and occult or visionary experience. LitCharts describes the speaker as Aengus, an Irish mythological god associated with youth, love, and poetic inspiration, while also noting that Yeats drew on both Irish tradition and a Greek folk song.

The poem adapts myth rather than simply retelling a single legend. Its Aengus is an aging wanderer whose encounter with a mysterious girl turns mythic identity into a human story of memory, pursuit, and frustrated desire.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of The Song of Wandering Aengus?

The poem presents a fleeting supernatural encounter as the source of a lifelong search. The girl can be read as lost love, an unattainable ideal, or a vision of beauty and creative inspiration.

### What are the main themes in The Song of Wandering Aengus?

Its main themes include longing, myth and transformation, the tension between aging and eternity, and the way an imagined ideal can shape an entire life.

### What meter does The Song of Wandering Aengus use?

The poem is mostly written in iambic tetrameter, with four main rising beats per line. Yeats varies the pattern to preserve a natural, songlike speaking voice.

### Who is Aengus in the poem?

Aengus is a figure from Irish mythology associated with youth, love, and poetic inspiration. Yeats uses him as the speaker of a personal, visionary story rather than retelling one myth in full.
