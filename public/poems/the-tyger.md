# The Tyger analysis

The Tyger — William Blake (1794)

This analysis of William Blake’s “The Tyger” explains how its relentless questions, forge imagery, and contrast with “The Lamb” make creation both beautiful and frightening.

## The poem

```
Tyger Tyger, burning bright,
In the forests of the night;
What immortal hand or eye,
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

In what distant deeps or skies.
Burnt the fire of thine eyes?
On what wings dare he aspire?
What the hand, dare seize the fire?

And what shoulder, & what art,
Could twist the sinews of thy heart?
And when thy heart began to beat.
What dread hand? & what dread feet?

What the hammer? what the chain,
In what furnace was thy brain?
What the anvil? what dread grasp.
Dare its deadly terrors clasp?

When the stars threw down their spears
And water'd heaven with their tears:
Did he smile his work to see?
Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

Tyger Tyger, burning bright,
In the forests of the night:
What immortal hand or eye,
Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?
```

Text from [The Tyger](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/43687/the-tyger). Public domain in the US (First published in Songs of Experience in 1794; William Blake died in 1827, so the poem is public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The speaker addresses the mysterious maker of a tiger whose beauty and violence are inseparable. Each stanza intensifies the question of how such a creature could be designed.

The poem never answers its questions. Its final stanza repeats the opening but changes “Could” to “Dare,” shifting attention from the creator’s ability to the audacity of making something so fearsome.

## Meaning and interpretation

The Tyger is not simply a symbol of evil. Its “fearful symmetry” joins order and terror, while the repeated questions leave its moral meaning unresolved. Blake makes uncertainty part of the poem’s subject.

The poem’s central theological problem is whether the same creator could make both the gentle Lamb and the dangerous Tyger. Rather than choosing one over the other, Blake places innocence and experience in a tense, mutually revealing relationship.

## Themes

### Creation and divine mystery

The speaker can imagine the Tyger’s design but cannot identify or understand its maker. The unanswered questions preserve creation as something larger than human explanation.

### Beauty and terror

“Burning bright” and “fearful symmetry” make the creature visually compelling, but the poem’s fire, dread, and deadly terrors keep admiration close to fear.

### Innocence and experience

The question “Did he who made the Lamb make thee?” directly connects this poem with “The Lamb.” Their different tones and creatures test whether a complete creation can contain both gentleness and force.

## Form and meter

The poem has six quatrains and is commonly read as trochaic tetrameter, often catalectic: four falling rhythmic units with the final unstressed syllable omitted. The hammering beat suits the forge imagery and the speaker’s urgency.

Most stanzas use rhyming couplets, though “eye” and “symmetry” can sound like a slant rhyme to modern readers. The repeated opening and closing stanzas create a circular structure, while “Could” becoming “Dare” marks the poem’s crucial change.

Nearly every line belongs to a question. The interrogative structure refuses a settled explanation and makes the reader experience the same uncertainty as the speaker.

## Literary devices

### Rhetorical questions

> What immortal hand or eye, / Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

The poem accumulates questions without providing answers. This turns uncertainty from a gap in the argument into its central formal effect.

### Forge imagery

> What the hammer? what the chain, / In what furnace was thy brain?

Hammer, chain, furnace, and anvil recast the creator as a laboring blacksmith. Creation becomes physical, forceful work rather than distant abstraction.

### Paradox

> Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

“Symmetry” suggests order and beauty, while “fearful” suggests danger. The paired terms hold attraction and terror together in one image.

### Repetition and variation

> Could frame thy fearful symmetry? ... Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?

The final stanza echoes the first almost exactly. The change from “Could” to “Dare” turns a question of capacity into a question of moral nerve.

## Historical context

Blake published “The Tyger” in Songs of Experience in 1794, the companion half of Songs of Innocence and of Experience. The collection’s subtitle presents the poems as “Two Contrary States of the Human Soul.”

Blake produced his books through illuminated printing, combining etched text and images and hand-colouring copies. The poem’s image of a maker working with fire and tools therefore resonates with Blake’s own identity as a poet, engraver, and visual artist.

The title’s spelling, “Tyger,” is retained from Blake’s text and should not be modernized to “Tiger.”

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of “The Tyger”?

The poem asks how a creator could make something that is both beautiful and terrifying. It does not resolve the question, presenting mystery and contradiction as central to creation.

### What does the Tyger symbolize?

The Tyger can symbolize fierce energy, danger, sublime power, or the difficult aspects of creation. Blake’s questions keep its meaning open rather than reducing it to simple evil.

### How does “The Tyger” contrast with “The Lamb”?

“The Lamb” asks who made the lamb and answers with childlike confidence. “The Tyger” asks whether the same maker created both animals and leaves the question unanswered, placing innocence beside experience.

### What is the poem’s meter?

The poem is commonly scanned as trochaic tetrameter, often with catalectic lines. Its falling, hammer-like rhythm reinforces the forge imagery and the repeated questioning.

### Why does Blake use forge imagery?

The hammer, chain, furnace, and anvil make creation seem physical and laborious. They present the maker as a powerful artisan who must shape dangerous energy into a living form.
