# The Walrus and the Carpenter analysis

The Walrus and the Carpenter — Lewis Carroll (1871)

This analysis of Lewis Carroll’s “The Walrus and the Carpenter” explains how its musical form and absurd story expose deception, appetite, and false sympathy.

## The poem

```
The sun was shining on the sea,
Shining with all his might:
He did his very best to make
The billows smooth and bright—
And this was odd, because it was
The middle of the night.

The moon was shining sulkily,
Because she thought the sun
Had got no business to be there
After the day was done—
“It’s very rude of him,” she said,
“To come and spoil the fun!”

The sea was wet as wet could be,
The sands were dry as dry.
You could not see a cloud, because
No cloud was in the sky:
No birds were flying overhead—
There were no birds to fly.

The Walrus and the Carpenter
Were walking close at hand;
They wept like anything to see
Such quantities of sand:
“If this were only cleared away,”
They said, “it would be grand!”

“If seven maids with seven mops
Swept it for half a year,
Do you suppose,” the Walrus said,
“That they could get it clear?”
“I doubt it,” said the Carpenter,
And shed a bitter tear.

“O Oysters, come and walk with us!”
The Walrus did beseech.
“A pleasant walk, a pleasant talk,
Along the briny beach:
We cannot do with more than four,
To give a hand to each.”

The eldest Oyster looked at him,
But never a word he said:
The eldest Oyster winked his eye,
And shook his heavy head—
Meaning to say he did not choose
To leave the oyster-bed.

But four young Oysters hurried up,
All eager for the treat:
Their coats were brushed, their faces washed,
Their shoes were clean and neat—
And this was odd, because, you know,
They hadn’t any feet.

Four other Oysters followed them,
And yet another four;
And thick and fast they came at last,
And more, and more, and more—
All hopping through the frothy waves,
And scrambling to the shore.

The Walrus and the Carpenter
Walked on a mile or so,
And then they rested on a rock
Conveniently low:
And all the little Oysters stood
And waited in a row.

“The time has come,” the Walrus said,
“To talk of many things:
Of shoes—and ships—and sealing-wax—
Of cabbages—and kings—
And why the sea is boiling hot—
And whether pigs have wings.”

“But wait a bit,” the Oysters cried,
“Before we have our chat;
For some of us are out of breath,
And all of us are fat!”
“No hurry!” said the Carpenter.
They thanked him much for that.

“A loaf of bread,” the Walrus said,
“Is what we chiefly need:
Pepper and vinegar besides
Are very good indeed—
Now if you are ready, Oysters dear,
We can begin to feed.”

“But not on us!” the Oysters cried,
Turning a little blue.
“After such kindness, that would be
A dismal thing to do!”
“The night is fine,” the Walrus said.
“Do you admire the view?”
“It was so kind of you to come!
And you are very nice!”

The Carpenter said nothing but
“Cut us another slice:
I wish you were not quite so deaf—
I’ve had to ask you twice!”

“It seems a shame,” the Walrus said,
“To play them such a trick,
After we’ve brought them out so far,
And made them trot so quick!”
The Carpenter said nothing but
“The butter’s spread too thick!”

“I weep for you,” the Walrus said.
“I deeply sympathize!”
With sobs and tears he sorted out
Those of the largest size,
Holding his pocket-handkerchief
Before his streaming eyes.

“O Oysters,” said the Carpenter,
“You’ve had a pleasant run!
Shall we be trotting home again?”
But answer came there none—
And this was scarcely odd, because
They’d eaten every one.
```

Text from [Through the Looking-Glass, Chapter IV](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Through_the_Looking-Glass,_and_What_Alice_Found_There/Chapter_IV). Public domain in the US (First published in Lewis Carroll’s Through the Looking-Glass in 1871, well before the United States public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

On an impossible moonlit beach, the Walrus and the Carpenter invite young Oysters to take a walk and talk. Their apparently kind invitation becomes a trap: the pair eat every Oyster who follows them.

The poem’s cheerful ballad music makes the ending both funny and disturbing. Its nonsense world does not erase the moral pattern of the story; it makes manipulation and appetite visible through comic exaggeration.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem presents predation as a performance. The Walrus uses sympathy, flattery, and distracting talk about “shoes—and ships—and sealing-wax” to keep the Oysters from recognizing the danger, while the Carpenter states the appetite more bluntly.

Carroll leaves the allegory open rather than assigning one fixed meaning to the characters. A grounded reading is that power can disguise exploitation as kindness, and that sentimental language is not proof of good intentions. The eldest Oyster’s refusal offers a small contrast: caution protects him where trust destroys the younger Oysters.

## Themes

### Deception and false sympathy

The Walrus cries over the Oysters and claims to sympathize with them even while selecting the largest to eat. Emotion becomes a mask that helps the predators manage their victims.

### Power and exploitation

The Oysters are persuaded, led away from safety, and consumed by figures with greater power. The absurd setting gives a simple food-chain relationship the shape of a social fable.

### Blind trust and caution

The young Oysters accept an attractive invitation, but the eldest Oyster notices the danger and stays in the oyster-bed. The contrast makes skepticism a form of survival rather than mere pessimism.

### Nonsense and dark humor

Talking celestial bodies, walking shellfish, and impossible shoes create a playful surface. That surface makes the final violence startling and lets the poem hold silliness and cruelty together.

## Form and meter

The poem has eighteen six-line stanzas, or sestets. The second, fourth, and sixth lines rhyme, creating an ABCBDB pattern that gives the narrative a familiar ballad shape.

The lines generally alternate iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter. Small variations, including the opening stress in “Shining,” keep the beat lively rather than mechanically regular.

The steady rhythm and repeated rhyme make the walk feel safe and nursery-rhyme-like, while the delayed revelation of the meal turns that musical security into irony.

## Literary devices

### Personification

> The moon was shining sulkily, / Because she thought the sun

The sun and moon behave like quarrelling people, establishing the poem’s impossible but emotionally recognizable world before the animal characters appear.

### Repetition and accumulation

> And more, and more, and more—

Repeated words turn the Oysters’ arrival into a comic flood. The abundance also quietly increases the scale of the impending meal.

### Bathos and irony

> “I weep for you,” the Walrus said. / “I deeply sympathize!”

Grand emotional language is placed beside the practical act of sorting and eating the Oysters. The mismatch exposes sympathy as theatrical and self-serving.

### Absurd imagery

> Their shoes were clean and neat— / And this was odd, because, you know, / They hadn’t any feet.

The joke invites the reader to accept impossible details while also teaching the poem’s method: logical contradiction is used to make the scene entertaining and unstable.

## Historical context

Lewis Carroll published the poem in Through the Looking-Glass in 1871. Tweedledum and Tweedledee recite it to Alice in Chapter IV, framing it as a performance inside the larger narrative.

The poem belongs to the Victorian nonsense tradition associated with Carroll and Edward Lear. Its formal regularity, wordplay, and impossible events allow it to work as both a children’s entertainment and a text open to darker interpretations.

The Walrus and the Carpenter have often attracted allegorical readings, but the poem does not require one-to-one symbols. Its clearest action is more general: persuasive language and apparent feeling can accompany exploitation.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of “The Walrus and the Carpenter”?

The poem shows how deception and appetite can hide behind friendliness, sympathy, and entertaining talk. Its nonsense style keeps the lesson comic without making the betrayal harmless.

### What are the main themes of the poem?

Its main themes include deception, exploitation, false sympathy, blind trust, and the uneasy relationship between playful nonsense and cruelty.

### What is the form and meter of “The Walrus and the Carpenter”?

It has eighteen six-line stanzas with an ABCBDB rhyme pattern. The lines generally alternate iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter, with occasional variations.

### Why does the eldest Oyster refuse to follow?

The eldest Oyster appears cautious and recognizes that the invitation may be dangerous. Unlike the younger Oysters, he does not trade the security of the oyster-bed for flattering promises.

### Are the Walrus and the Carpenter symbols?

They can support allegorical readings about power, greed, or hypocrisy, but Carroll’s poem does not provide a single required code. Their behavior matters even without assigning each character a fixed real-world counterpart.
