# The Walrus and the Carpenter analysis

The Walrus and the Carpenter — Lewis Carroll (1871)

Carroll’s cheerful nonsense story uses musical form and predatory comedy to expose false sympathy and exploitation.

Full text from [Through the Looking-Glass, Chapter IV](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Through_the_Looking-Glass,_and_What_Alice_Found_There/Chapter_IV). Public domain in the US (Published in Through the Looking-Glass in 1871 and public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The Walrus and the Carpenter invite young Oysters for a pleasant walk, then eat them all.[2]

```
“O Oysters, come and walk with us!”
The Walrus did beseech.
“A pleasant walk, a pleasant talk,
```

The poem’s nursery-like music makes the betrayal comic on the surface and disturbing underneath.[3]

## Meaning and interpretation

The Walrus uses flattery, sympathy, and distracting talk to manage the Oysters, while the Carpenter states the appetite more directly.[2]

```
“A loaf of bread,” the Walrus said,
“Is what we chiefly need:
Pepper and vinegar besides
```

The eldest Oyster’s refusal offers a small contrast: caution protects him where trust destroys the younger Oysters.[4]

## Themes

### Deception and false sympathy

```
“I weep for you,” the Walrus said.
“I deeply sympathize!”
```

Emotional language becomes a mask for predation.[2]

### Power and exploitation

The stronger characters lead vulnerable creatures away from safety and consume them.

### Nonsense and dark humor

```
Their shoes were brushed and clean—
And this was odd, because, you know,
They hadn’t any feet.
```

Logical contradiction keeps the scene playful while making its violence stranger.

## Form and meter

The poem uses eighteen six-line stanzas with a recurring ballad-like ABCBDB rhyme pattern.[3]

Alternating tetrameter and trimeter make the walk feel safe and songlike, which sharpens the irony of the ending.

## Literary devices

### Personification

```
The moon was shining sulkily,
Because she thought the sun
```

Talking celestial bodies establish an impossible but emotionally recognizable world.

### Bathos and irony

```
“I weep for you,” the Walrus said.
“I deeply sympathize!”
```

Grand feeling sits beside practical eating, exposing sympathy as theatrical.

### Accumulation

```
And thick and fast they came at last,
And more, and more, and more—
```

Repetition turns the Oysters’ arrival into a comic flood and increases the scale of the meal.

## Historical context

The poem appears in Chapter IV of Through the Looking-Glass, where Tweedledum and Tweedledee recite it to Alice.[1]

It belongs to Victorian nonsense verse; criticism cautions against forcing the characters into one fixed allegory.[4]

## Common questions

### What is the poem’s main meaning?

It shows how appetite and exploitation can hide behind friendliness, sympathy, and entertaining talk.

### What are its themes?

Deception, power, false sympathy, blind trust, and the uneasy mixture of nonsense with cruelty.

### What is its form?

A ballad-like poem of eighteen six-line stanzas with alternating tetrameter and trimeter.
