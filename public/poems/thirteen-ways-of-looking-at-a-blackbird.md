# Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird analysis

Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird — Wallace Stevens (1917)

Wallace Stevens's Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird presents thirteen brief encounters with one bird. Its meaning emerges through shifting perspectives rather than a single fixed symbol.

## The poem

```
I
Among twenty snowy mountains,
The only moving thing
Was the eye of the blackbird.

II
I was of three minds,
Like a tree
In which there are three blackbirds.

III
The blackbird whirled in the autumn winds.
It was a small part of the pantomime.

IV
A man and a woman
Are one.
A man and a woman and a blackbird
Are one.

V
I do not know which to prefer,
The beauty of inflections
Or the beauty of innuendoes,
The blackbird whistling
Or just after.

VI
Icicles filled the long window
With barbaric glass.
The shadow of the blackbird
Crossed it, to and fro.
The mood
Traced in the shadow
An indecipherable cause.

VII
O thin men of Haddam,
Why do you imagine golden birds?
Do you not see how the blackbird
Walks around the feet
Of the women about you?

VIII
I know noble accents
And lucid, inescapable rhythms;
But I know, too,
That the blackbird is involved
In what I know.

IX
When the blackbird flew out of sight,
It marked the edge
Of one of many circles.

X
At the sight of blackbirds
Flying in a green light,
Even the bawds of euphony
Would cry out sharply.

XI
He rode over Connecticut
In a glass coach.
Once, a fear pierced him,
In that he mistook
The shadow of his equipage
For blackbirds.

XII
The river is moving.
The blackbird must be flying.

XIII
It was evening all afternoon.
It was snowing
And it was going to snow.
The blackbird sat
In the cedar-limbs.
```

Text from [Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird — Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45236/thirteen-ways-of-looking-at-a-blackbird). Public domain in the US (First published in Others in 1917; works first published in the United States by 1930 are public domain.).

## Summary

The poem consists of thirteen numbered sections, each placing a blackbird in a different visual, emotional, or intellectual setting. Snowy mountains, autumn wind, a window, a Connecticut road, a river, and cedar branches become distinct frames for perception.

The sections do not build one continuous narrative. Instead, they accumulate sensations and contrasts: movement and stillness, sound and silence, nature and imagination, and the ordinary bird and the meanings observers project onto it.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem's central meaning is that seeing is never neutral. The blackbird changes as the speaker's situation changes: it can be an eye, part of a mind, a shadow, a sound, a boundary, or simply a bird sitting in a tree. The poem therefore asks how knowledge and beauty are shaped by attention.

The blackbird should not be reduced to one symbol such as death or freedom. As Oliver Tearle notes, the poem offers the bird as “a symbol, as image, as thing in the world,” keeping several kinds of reality in play. The closing stanza reverses the opening image: snow now moves while the blackbird is still, showing that even the relation between observer, object, and world can change.

## Themes

### Perception and perspective

Each section supplies a new angle of vision, from the eye among snowy mountains to the blackbird's shadow on glass. No single viewpoint cancels the others; together they show reality as plural and situated.

### Imagination and reality

The poem moves between a physical bird and the meanings attached to it. The “golden birds” imagined by the thin men of Haddam contrast with the blackbird walking among actual people, while the glass coach turns a shadow into a source of fear.

### Nature and human knowledge

The bird is not merely outside the speaker's mind. It is “involved” in what the speaker knows, linking natural images to language, rhythm, mood, and thought.

### Change and stillness

Wind, flying, rivers, and snow create motion, but the eye, shadow, and seated bird also pause the scene. The final section deliberately inverts the movement of the first, making change a matter of viewpoint as well as weather.

## Form and meter

The poem is made of thirteen numbered sections rather than a continuous stanzaic argument. The sections range from two lines to seven lines, and their abrupt shifts resemble a sequence of lyric snapshots.

The lines use free verse and do not maintain a regular meter or end-rhyme scheme. Short declarative sentences, repeated syntax, and carefully placed pauses create rhythm without a fixed metrical pattern.

Several sections have the compression and image-centered focus associated with haiku, though the poem is not a traditional haiku sequence. Its form makes each observation feel briefly complete while the whole poem keeps revising what the blackbird can mean.

## Literary devices

### Juxtaposition

> Among twenty snowy mountains,

The blackbird's dark, moving eye is set against an immense white and nearly motionless landscape. Later sections repeat this method by placing the bird beside icicles, green light, water, and snow.

### Repetition and variation

> A man and a woman / Are one.

Repeated structures such as “I know” and “The blackbird” create continuity across the fragments. Small changes in the repeated pattern alter its meaning, as when the blackbird is added to the man and woman.

### Paradox

> It was evening all afternoon.

The compressed contradiction collapses ordinary time and prepares the final scene's dreamlike stillness. The poem repeatedly makes perception feel both precise and unstable.

### Metonymy and shadow imagery

> The shadow of the blackbird / Crossed it, to and fro.

The shadow becomes an indirect presence of the bird and prompts an unexplained mood. In the glass coach, a different shadow is mistaken for blackbirds, showing how perception can create fear from partial evidence.

## Historical context

“Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird” first appeared in the modernist magazine Others in 1917 and was later collected in Harmonium in 1923. Its short, imagistic sections belong to the period's interest in fractured viewpoints and concentrated images.

The Poetry Foundation describes the poem as “highly reminiscent of haiku” and places it among the poems in Harmonium, a collection marked by precise imagery, unusual vocabulary, and philosophical attention to perception. Stevens's wider poetry often explores the relation between creative imagination and objective reality.

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning of Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird?

The poem explores how perception shapes meaning. Its blackbird changes across thirteen settings, so the poem resists one final interpretation and shows reality through multiple viewpoints.

### What are the main themes in the poem?

Its main themes include perception, imagination and reality, nature and knowledge, and the tension between movement and stillness.

### What form and meter does the poem use?

It is a sequence of thirteen irregular, imagistic sections written in free verse. It has no consistent meter or rhyme scheme, though repetition and short lines give it a deliberate rhythm.

### Is the blackbird a symbol of one specific idea?

Not necessarily. The poem presents the blackbird as a changing object of attention: sometimes physical, sometimes imagined, and sometimes bound up with the speaker's knowledge.
