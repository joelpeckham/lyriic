# Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird analysis

Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird — Wallace Stevens (1917)

Stevens presents thirteen brief encounters with one bird, making meaning emerge through shifting perspectives rather than one symbol.

Full text from [Thirteen Ways of Looking at a Blackbird](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45236/thirteen-ways-of-looking-at-a-blackbird). Public domain in the US (First published in 1917 and public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

Thirteen numbered sections place a blackbird in natural, social, visual, and intellectual settings.[2]

```
Among twenty snowy mountains,
The only moving thing
Was the eye of the blackbird.
```

The poem accumulates sensations rather than presenting one continuous narrative or fixed meaning.[3]

## Meaning and interpretation

The blackbird changes with the observer: it can be an eye, a shadow, a sound, a boundary, or simply a bird.[2]

```
I do not know which to prefer,
The beauty of inflections
Or the beauty of innuendoes,
```

The closing section reverses the opening: snow moves while the blackbird is still, showing perception as relational and changeable.[3]

## Themes

### Perception and perspective

Each section supplies a new angle; no single viewpoint cancels the others.[2]

### Imagination and reality

```
Why do you imagine golden birds?
Do you not see how the blackbird
Walks around the feet
```

Imagined golden birds contrast with attention to the ordinary bird at hand.

### Change and stillness

```
It was evening all afternoon.
It was snowing
And it was going to snow.
```

The final temporal instability prepares the still blackbird in the cedar limbs.

## Form and meter

The poem consists of thirteen numbered sections ranging from two to seven lines.[3]

It uses free verse rather than fixed meter or end rhyme; pauses, repetition, and compressed images create its rhythm.[4]

## Literary devices

### Juxtaposition

```
Among twenty snowy mountains,
The only moving thing
```

Dark movement against white stillness makes perception immediate.

### Paradox

```
It was evening all afternoon.
```

The contradiction makes ordinary time feel unstable.

### Repetition and variation

```
A man and a woman
Are one.
A man and a woman and a blackbird
```

The added bird changes the meaning of the repeated declaration.

## Historical context

The poem first appeared in Others in 1917 and was later collected in Harmonium (1923).[3]

Its short, haiku-like sections reflect modernist interest in concentrated images and multiple viewpoints.[4]

## Common questions

### What is the main meaning?

The poem shows that perception shapes meaning and resists one final interpretation of the bird.

### What are its themes?

Perspective, imagination and reality, nature and knowledge, movement, and stillness.

### What form does it use?

Thirteen irregular free-verse sections with haiku-like compression.
