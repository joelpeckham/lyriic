# To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time analysis

To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time — Robert Herrick (1648)

Herrick’s carpe diem lyric turns fading flowers and the setting sun into an urgent argument about youth and marriage.

Full text from [First-edition transcription](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/work/Herrick/herrick-virgins). Public domain in the US (First published in Hesperides in 1648 and public domain in the United States.).

## Summary

The speaker urges young women to act while youth, beauty, and romantic opportunity remain.[2]

```
Gather ye Rose-buds while ye may,
Old Time is still a-flying;
And this same flower that smiles today,
```

The poem moves from rose to sun to aging, then ends with a direct command to marry.[3]

## Meaning and interpretation

The lyric’s carpe diem argument makes decline part of beauty itself: the flower smiles while already moving toward death.[4]

```
The higher he’s a-getting;
The sooner will his Race be run,
And nearer he’s to Setting.
```

Its marriage advice reflects its period but also creates a gendered pressure that modern readers can question.[2]

## Themes

### Passing time

Time is an active force, so delay consumes the very opportunity the poem urges its audience to use.[2]

### Beauty and decay

```
And this same flower that smiles today,
To morrow will be dying.
```

The rose makes youth and decline simultaneous.

### Marriage and social pressure

```
Then be not coy, but use your time;
And while ye may, go marry:
```

The final imperative narrows broad advice about pleasure into a historically specific prescription.

## Form and meter

The sixteen-line poem has four quatrains with alternating ABAB rhyme.[3]

Its common-meter movement alternates four and three stresses, making the warning sound buoyant and memorable.

## Literary devices

### Extended natural imagery

```
Gather ye Rose-buds while ye may,
Old Time is still a-flying;
```

Flower and sun translate abstract time into visible cycles.

### Personification

```
Old Time is still a-flying;
```

Time becomes ancient yet alarmingly quick.

### Paradox

```
The higher he’s a-getting;
The sooner will his Race be run,
```

The sun’s ascent is also its approach to setting.

## Historical context

Herrick published the poem as number 208 in Hesperides in 1648, during political unrest and the English Civil War.[2]

The poem combines classical carpe diem with Christian marriage, a distinctive tension in Herrick’s Cavalier context.[2]

## Common questions

### What is the poem’s meaning?

It urges readers to use the present because youth, beauty, and life are temporary.

### What are its themes?

Passing time, beauty and decay, carpe diem, marriage, and social pressure.

### What form does it use?

Four rhyming quatrains with common-meter movement.
