# To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time analysis

To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time — Robert Herrick (1648)

This analysis explains the meaning and interpretation of Robert Herrick's famous carpe diem lyric, from its rosebud imagery to its urgent final advice.

## The poem

```
Gather ye Rose-buds while ye may,
Old Time is still a-flying;
And this same flower that smiles today,
To morrow will be dying.

The glorious Lamp of Heaven, the Sun,
The higher he’s a-getting;
The sooner will his Race be run,
And nearer he’s to Setting.

That Age is best, which is the first,
When Youth and Blood are warmer;
But being spent, the worse, and worst
Times, still succeed the former.

Then be not coy, but use your time;
And while ye may, go marry:
For having lost but once your prime,
You may forever tarry.
```

Text from [First-edition transcription](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/work/Herrick/herrick-virgins). Public domain in the US (First published in 1648 in Herrick's Hesperides, well before the 1930 US public-domain cutoff.).

## Summary

The speaker addresses young women and urges them to act while youth, beauty, and romantic opportunity are still available. The opening rosebuds compress the life of a flower into a brief interval: what smiles today will be dying tomorrow.

The poem then moves from the flower to the sun, from natural cycles to human aging, and finally to a direct command: do not delay marriage. Its cheerful, song-like surface carries a darker warning about irreversible loss.

## Meaning and interpretation

The poem's central meaning is a version of carpe diem: time is always moving, so the present should not be wasted. The rose and the setting sun make decline part of the very moment of beauty, rather than a separate event that arrives later.

Herrick does not leave the classical invitation to pleasure entirely unbounded. As the Encyclopedia.com overview explains, the poem joins the natural cycle of life and death to Christian marriage. A modern reading should also notice the pressure in the speaker's argument: it treats women's youth and desirability as limited social resources, which makes the warning both historically revealing and ethically contestable.

## Themes

### Carpe diem and passing time

“Old Time is still a-flying” turns the familiar call to seize the day into a race against an ancient, constant force. The poem insists that delay is not neutral: every delay consumes part of the time it asks the virgins to use.

### Beauty, youth, and decay

The rosebud, the sun, and warm blood all represent vitality, but each image already contains its decline. Herrick's argument depends on the paradox that the closer something comes to its height, the closer it is to setting or dying.

### Marriage, gender, and social pressure

The final command changes the poem from broad advice about pleasure to a specifically gendered prescription. Its historical setting helps explain the marriage advice, but readers can still question the speaker's assumption that a woman's future depends on preserving or spending youthful beauty.

### Classical pleasure and Christian order

The sensual rosebud and sun imagery recalls classical carpe diem poetry, while “go marry” places desire within a socially sanctioned Christian institution. The poem's tension comes from holding both impulses together.

## Form and meter

The lyric has sixteen lines arranged as four quatrains, with an alternating ABAB rhyme pattern: may/today, a-flying/dying, and so on. Each stanza is a single sentence, so the argument flows continuously from image to command.

The poem uses common-meter movement: the first and third lines generally carry four stresses, while the second and fourth carry three. Its compact, hymn-like rhythm and regular rhyme make the warning sound buoyant even as the subject is death and irreversible aging.

## Literary devices

### Extended natural imagery

> And this same flower that smiles today, / To morrow will be dying.

The rosebud and the sun translate abstract time into visible cycles. Their beauty and decline give the speaker a concrete model for human youth.

### Personification

> Old Time is still a-flying

Time becomes an active, unstoppable figure. The combination of “Old” and “a-flying” makes it both ancient and alarmingly quick.

### Paradox

> The higher he’s a-getting; / The sooner will his Race be run

The sun's ascent is also its approach to setting. Herrick uses this apparent contradiction to argue that the height of youth is inseparable from the beginning of decline.

### Imperative voice and end rhyme

> Then be not coy, but use your time; / And while ye may, go marry:

Commands turn observation into pressure. The final rhyme of “marry” and “tarry” gives the closing warning a memorable, almost proverbial snap.

## Historical context

Herrick published the poem as number 208 in Hesperides in 1648, during the English Civil War. The Encyclopedia.com overview identifies Herrick as a Cavalier poet and notes that he was also a clergyman, context that helps explain the poem's combination of sensual imagery and marriage as a moral boundary.

The poem belongs to the long carpe diem tradition associated with Horace and to the lighter, more musical Cavalier style. Its language is not a neutral description of romance: the speaker addresses “virgins” through the social expectations of seventeenth-century England, including the fear of unmarried women losing status and opportunity.

## Common questions

### What is the meaning of “To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time”?

It is a carpe diem poem urging young women not to waste their youth and romantic opportunities because time, beauty, and life are temporary.

### What are the main themes of the poem?

Its main themes are the rapid passage of time, the transience of beauty, carpe diem, marriage, and the tension between sensual pleasure and Christian social order.

### What is the poem's form and meter?

It is a sixteen-line lyric in four rhyming quatrains with an ABAB pattern. The lines generally alternate four and three stresses in common-meter fashion.

### What does the rosebud symbolize?

The rosebud symbolizes youth, beauty, romantic possibility, and the brief period before those qualities fade.
